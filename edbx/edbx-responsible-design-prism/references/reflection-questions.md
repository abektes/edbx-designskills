# Reflection Questions — Facilitation Guide

Expanded guidance for facilitating the four reflection prompts in the Responsible Design Prism.

The reflection prompts are the heart of the method. Scoring the axes is diagnostic; reflecting on the scores is transformative. Do not rush these. Allow silence. Let discomfort sit.

---

## Prompt 1: Self-Reflection

**"Where do you stand when you build? What state do you seek?"**

### Purpose
Surface the designer's internal state, assumptions, and intentions. This prompt shifts attention from the artifact to the person making it.

### Facilitation guidance

Ask the designer or team to consider:

- What were you optimizing for when you designed this? Be specific — was it conversion? Engagement? Task completion? Something else?
- Did you think about the user's goals at the same time as the business goals? Or did one dominate?
- What would you have designed if user wellbeing were the only metric that mattered?
- What pressure were you under when you made this decision? Timeline? Metric target? Manager request?
- If a friend or family member used this feature, would you feel comfortable? If not, what specifically concerns you?

### What to listen for

- Defensiveness is data. If the team is defensive about a design decision, that discomfort points to something worth examining.
- "We didn't have time" or "leadership wanted it" are system-level observations, not excuses. Name them as organizational dynamics, not personal failures.
- If the answer is "I didn't think about it," that is the most honest and most useful response. Unconscious design is the most common source of dark patterns.

### Common pitfalls

- Do not let this become a therapy session. The goal is awareness, not confession.
- Do not accept "I just followed the spec" as a final answer. Follow up: "And when you read the spec, what did you think?"

## Prompt 2: Organization Ecology

**"What org pressures push toward dark patterns?"**

### Purpose
Map the system dynamics that shape design decisions. Individual designers rarely create dark patterns alone — organizations create the conditions for them.

### Facilitation guidance

Ask the team to consider:

- What metrics does your team report on? Which ones would improve if the design were more exploitative?
- Has anyone on the team ever raised an ethical concern about a design? What happened?
- What happens when user research findings conflict with business targets?
- Is there an ethical review in your design or product process? If not, why not?
- What would leadership say if you proposed removing a feature that drives engagement but harms users?

### What to listen for

- Organizational reward structures. If people are promoted based on engagement growth, dark patterns are rational career moves. That tension is the real problem.
- Cultural norms. "That's just how we do it here" often normalizes patterns that would be flagged in a different context.
- Resource allocation. If ethical review never gets time in the sprint, it is not actually valued — regardless of what the mission statement says.

### Common pitfalls

- Do not let this become org-bashing. The goal is understanding system dynamics, not assigning blame.
- Do not let "we can't change the org" end the conversation. The prism generates redesign suggestions that work within constraints.

## Prompt 3: Transition

**"How can responsible design patterns help?"**

### Purpose
Move from diagnosis to action. For every Red or Yellow axis, generate a concrete path toward Green.

### Facilitation guidance

For each flagged axis, ask:

- What is the smallest change that would move this from Red/Yellow toward Green?
- What responsible design pattern could replace the current pattern? (See the taxonomy for alternatives.)
- What would the transitional version look like? Not perfect — just better.
- Has any team or product solved this same tension well? What did they do?
- What would you need to make this change? Engineering time? Design research? Leadership support?

### What to listen for

- Concrete suggestions over abstract ideals. "Make the cancel button visible" is useful. "Be more user-centered" is not.
- Incremental paths. The redesign does not have to be perfect. Moving from Red to Yellow is progress.
- Business-aligned framing. The more the redesign suggestion can be framed in business terms, the more likely it is to be adopted.

### Common pitfalls

- Do not demand perfection. A design that moves one axis from Red to Yellow is better than a design that stays Red waiting for a perfect solution.
- Do not suggest alternatives that ignore business constraints. Responsible design has to work within real organizational limits.

## Prompt 4: Recovery

**"How do you find your way back to the right side?"**

### Purpose
Address designs that are already shipped and causing harm. Recovery is different from prevention — it requires acknowledging damage and charting a path back.

### Facilitation guidance

Ask the team to consider:

- If this pattern is already live, what is its effect right now on real users?
- What would a responsible version of this feature look like? Describe it in detail.
- Can the dark pattern be removed without breaking the business model? If not, what does that reveal about the business model?
- What is the cost of inaction? User churn, reputation damage, regulatory risk, team morale?
- What would you say to a user who discovered this pattern and asked why it was designed this way?

### What to listen for

- Acknowledgment of harm. If the team cannot name specific harm, they have not looked closely enough.
- Fear of business impact. This is legitimate and should be addressed with the business-framing reference — not dismissed.
- Historical context. Sometimes the team inherited the pattern and feels powerless. Recovery starts with understanding how the pattern got there.

### Common pitfalls

- Do not minimize harm that has already occurred. Acknowledge it directly.
- Do not suggest recovery without acknowledging the need for it. The "sorry we got caught" tone is worse than silence.
- Do not promise outcomes you cannot deliver. Recovery is a process, not a single redesign.

---

## General Facilitation Notes

- The four prompts work best in order. Self-reflection opens the door, organization ecology explains the system, transition builds the path, recovery addresses what is already broken.
- Allow 5–10 minutes per prompt in a workshop setting. Do not rush.
- If the team is new to ethical design discussions, start with Prompt 1 and go slowly. If they are experienced, you can move faster but still hit all four.
- Document answers as you go. The redesign pathways in Step 4 draw directly from these reflections.
- If a prompt generates strong emotion, let it. Discomfort is not a sign the exercise is failing — it is a sign it is working.
