# Prism Axes — Full Definitions

Reference for the five spectrum axes used in the Responsible Design Prism.

---

## Axis 1: User Relationship

**Dark pole: User as Means**
The design treats the user as a resource to be extracted from — a source of clicks, data, engagement, revenue, or growth. The user's goals are secondary to the business's goals. The user is the product, not the customer.

Indicators:
- Metrics track extraction (sessions, time-on-app, ad views) rather than outcomes (task completion, satisfaction, goal achievement)
- User research focuses on how to increase engagement, not how to serve user needs
- The design optimizes for the business metric even when it conflicts with the user's stated intent
- Users are segmented by revenue potential, not by need

**Responsible pole: User as Themselves**
The design treats the user as a person with their own goals, context, and agency. The design serves the user's intent, even when it means the user leaves sooner, pays less, or engages less frequently.

Indicators:
- Metrics include user outcome measures (task success, time-to-value, satisfaction)
- The design supports the user in completing their task and leaving
- User research centers on understanding needs, not optimizing extraction
- The product respects the user's time, attention, and autonomy

**Transitional zone:**
The design does not actively exploit users but has not fully aligned with their interests either. Common in products where engagement metrics are tracked but not weaponized, or where user research exists but doesn't drive decisions.

## Axis 2: Information

**Dark pole: Setting False Expectations**
The design presents information in a way that creates an inaccurate mental model. The user believes they understand what will happen, but the reality is different — and the design benefits from that misunderstanding.

Indicators:
- Visual hierarchy emphasizes the business-preferred interpretation
- Critical information is hidden in fine print, collapsed sections, or below the fold
- Copy uses ambiguity or hedging that the user will not notice
- Pricing, terms, or consequences are presented in misleading contexts

**Responsible pole: Transparent Communication**
The design presents information so the user can form an accurate mental model before acting. Key information is visible, clear, and available at the point of decision — not buried or deferred.

Indicators:
- Pricing includes all costs before the user commits
- Terms are summarized in plain language at the point of action
- Visual hierarchy reflects what matters to the user, not just what benefits the business
- The design helps the user understand trade-offs rather than obscuring them

**Transitional zone:**
Information is technically present but not easily accessible. Fine print exists but is not maliciously hidden. The user can find the truth if they look carefully — but the design does not go out of its way to surface it.

---

## Axis 3: Error Handling

**Dark pole: Taking Advantage of Errors**
The design benefits when users make mistakes. Errors are not prevented, not corrected, and sometimes even encouraged — because the resulting action serves the business.

Indicators:
- Accidental clicks lead to irreversible business-favorable actions
- No confirmation step for consequential actions (purchases, subscriptions, data sharing)
- Error messages are vague or anxiety-inducing rather than helpful
- The "undo" window is absent or too short for consequential actions
- Default states are set to maximize business value when the user skips or errs

**Responsible pole: Inform and Support Recovery**
The design anticipates common errors, prevents them where possible, and helps the user recover when they happen. Error states are treated as design problems, not user failures.

Indicators:
- Confirmation dialogs for consequential actions, written in plain language
- Generous undo windows
- Clear, specific error messages that tell the user what went wrong and how to fix it
- Auto-save, auto-recover, and graceful degradation
- Default states favor user safety over business optimization

**Transitional zone:**
Errors are not weaponized but recovery is harder than it should be. Confirmation dialogs exist but use confusing language. Error messages are present but not actionable. The design does not exploit mistakes but also does not actively prevent them.

---

## Axis 4: Business Alignment

**Dark pole: Exploitative Incentives**
The business model or incentive structure directly rewards dark patterns. Designers are measured on engagement, conversion, or revenue metrics that conflict with user wellbeing, and the organization does not provide guardrails.

Indicators:
- Performance reviews tied to dark-adjacent metrics (time-on-app, ad clicks, upsell rate)
- No ethical review in the design or product process
- Leadership rewards short-term growth over long-term trust
- Business model depends on user behavior that the user would not choose deliberately

**Responsible pole: Fair Practice Guidelines**
The organization has explicit guidelines that align business incentives with user outcomes. Design decisions are evaluated against both business and user wellbeing metrics. There is organizational support for choosing the ethical path.

Indicators:
- Published design principles or ethical guidelines
- Product metrics include trust, satisfaction, and outcome measures alongside growth
- Ethical review is part of the shipping process
- Leadership supports trade-offs that favor user trust over short-term gain
- Business model alignment — the product makes money when users succeed

**Transitional zone:**
Guidelines exist on paper but are not enforced. The team wants to do the right thing but lacks organizational backing. Metrics are mixed — some user-centered, some extraction-focused. Individual designers make ethical choices but the system does not require or reward them.

---

## Axis 5: Usability

**Dark pole: Weaponized Usability**
Usability techniques are used to make the business-preferred path effortless and the user-preferred path difficult. The design is usable — but in service of extraction, not user goals.

Indicators:
- The "upgrade" button is large, prominent, and one click; the "skip" link is small, low-contrast, and requires scrolling
- Navigation is designed to prevent the user from finding cancellation or opt-out options
- The easy path always benefits the business; the user-favorable path requires effort
- Animation and visual design create urgency or emotional pressure

**Responsible pole: Genuine Usability**
Usability serves the user's goals. The most usable path is the one that best serves the user — even when it means easier cancellation, fewer conversions, or less engagement.

Indicators:
- The primary action is the one the user came to do, not the one the business prefers
- Cancel, delete, and opt-out are as usable as sign up and upgrade
- Visual hierarchy reflects user priorities, not business priorities
- The design reduces cognitive load for the user's task, not for the conversion funnel

**Transitional zone:**
Usability is generally good but has inherited patterns that nudge toward business interests. The sign-up flow is polished; the cancellation flow is functional but not designed with the same care. Visual hierarchy is mostly neutral with occasional business-biased emphasis.
