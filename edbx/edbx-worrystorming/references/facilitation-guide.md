# Facilitation Guide — Running Worrystorming as a Team Workshop

This guide covers how to facilitate a Worrystorming session with a group — from setup to synthesis.

---

## Setup

**Group size:** 3–8 participants is ideal. Larger groups should split into parallel sessions and share results.

**Time needed:**
- Quick scan: 30 minutes (Steps 2–3 only)
- Full session: 60–90 minutes
- Deep workshop: 2 hours (includes discussion and commitment-setting)

**Materials:**
- Sticky notes (physical or digital — Miro, FigJam, or equivalent)
- A shared surface visible to all participants
- Markers or digital text tool
- Timer

**Roles:**
- Facilitator: guides the process, manages time, ensures coverage of all eight categories
- Participants: generate worries, cluster, reframe — ideally a mix of roles (design, product, engineering, research, support)

---

## Step-by-Step Facilitation

### Phase 1: Worry Generation (10–15 minutes)

1. **Frame the exercise.** Explain that the goal is exhaustive concern-surfacing, not problem-solving. Emphasize that naming a worry is not an accusation — it is an act of responsible design.

2. **Silent writing (5 minutes).** Each participant writes one worry per sticky note. No discussion during this phase — discussion anchors the group on the first ideas spoken aloud.

3. **Round-robin sharing (5–10 minutes).** Each person reads one worry aloud and places it on the shared surface. Continue rounds until all worries are visible. Do not evaluate, debate, or dismiss during sharing.

4. **Category check.** The facilitator scans the worries against the eight concern categories. Flag any categories with no worries: "I notice we don't have any worries about 🌍 Environmental Impact or ⚖️ Power & Fairness. Let's take 2 more minutes of silent writing focused on those gaps."

5. **Aim for 10–20 total worries minimum** from a group of 4–6 people.

### Phase 2: Clustering (10–15 minutes)

1. **Silent sorting (3 minutes).** Participants physically (or digitally) group related worries into clusters on the shared surface. Multiple people can move notes simultaneously.

2. **Name the clusters.** For each group of worries, collaboratively choose a name. Encourage affirmative naming: "Data Sovereignty" rather than "Data Panic." The name is the first step of reframing.

3. **Severity tagging.** For each cluster, the group votes on severity:
   - 🔴 Critical: likely, severe, and unaddressed
   - 🟡 Monitor: possible, moderate, or partially addressed
   - 🟢 Low Risk: unlikely, minor, or well-mitigated

   Quick method: each participant holds up fingers (🔴 = 3, 🟡 = 2, 🟢 = 1) and the facilitator reads the consensus.

### Phase 3: Reframing (15–20 minutes)

1. **Work through each 🔴 Critical cluster first.** For each, collaboratively draft:
   - Ethical Objective: "We commit to [X] in order to avoid [Y]."
   - Design Constraint: A specific, testable rule
   - Test Question: "How would we know if we failed?"

2. **Then address 🟡 Monitor clusters.** Same format, but quicker — these may not need full treatment.

3. **🟢 Low Risk clusters** get a brief note: what's already in place that keeps the risk low, and what could change that.

### Phase 4: Mitigations (10–15 minutes)

For each 🔴 Critical cluster:
1. Propose 1–2 concrete mitigations or design alternatives
2. Flag which current design decisions are most at risk from this worry
3. Name who should be consulted (affected community, legal, domain expert)

### Phase 5: Summary (5–10 minutes)

The facilitator drafts a **Worrystorm Summary** — a single paragraph that:
- Names the product evaluated
- Lists the critical clusters surfaced
- States the core ethical challenge in plain language
- Identifies the most important value commitment

Read it aloud. Ask: "Does this capture what happened? Did I miss anything?"

## Facilitation Tips

**Energy management:** Worrystorming can feel heavy — surfacing concerns about your own product is uncomfortable. The reframing step (Phase 3) is the release valve. Make sure you get to it. Ending on raw worries without values leaves the group in a worse state than when they started.

**Dominant voices:** If one person is generating most of the worries, that's a signal that others are filtering. Extend the silent writing phase. Ask specifically for "a worry you haven't said out loud yet."

**The "this would never happen" dismissal:** When someone dismisses a worry as unrealistic, ask: "If it did happen, how bad would it be?" Low-probability, high-severity events are often the most important to surface.

**Category coverage:** Keep the eight-category taxonomy visible during the session. When the group has covered 6 of 8 categories, gently redirect: "We've been thorough on data and safety. What about economic and future consequences?"

**Psychological safety:** Start by normalizing the exercise. "Every product has ethical concerns. The absence of concerns means we haven't looked hard enough, not that we've built something perfect."

---

## Adapting for Different Stages

**Ideation stage:** Focus on 🔮 Future Consequences and ⚖️ Power & Fairness — these are hardest to fix later.

**In-development:** Focus on 🧠 Psychological and 👥 Community & Social — the design is taking shape and these concerns are still addressable.

**Pre-launch:** Focus on 🔒 Safety & Harm and 🗃️ Data & Privacy — these are regulatory and reputational risks that must be resolved before shipping.

**Live product:** Focus on all categories equally — the product is already affecting people. Consider running edb-worrystorming quarterly as a living practice.
