# Motivation Types — Reference Definitions

Use these definitions when filling matrix cells. Each type describes a core human drive that design can support, nudge, or exploit.

---

## 1. Achievement

**Core drive:** The desire to make progress, master a skill, complete a task, or reach a defined goal.

**What it looks like in design:**
- Progress bars and completion percentages
- Badges, trophies, and unlockable achievements
- Streaks and consistency counters
- Skill trees and level-up systems
- "You completed X!" notifications

**When it supports users:**
- The goal is genuinely the user's own. The user chose it, understands it, and can change it.
- Progress feedback helps the user understand where they are relative to their own intent.
- Mastery is real — the user actually gets better at something they value.

**When it becomes manipulative:**
- The product defines goals the user did not choose and makes them feel like failures for not meeting them.
- Progress mechanics are designed to be endless — there is always another level, another badge, another milestone — so the user never feels "done."
- Streaks are framed so that missing a day feels like loss rather than a neutral event.
- Achievement signals are public by default, turning personal progress into social performance.

**Examples:**
- 🟢 A language app that tracks words learned and lets the user set their own daily target.
- 🟡 A fitness app that sends "You're falling behind your group!" notifications.
- 🔴 A productivity tool that makes incomplete tasks visually punishing (red, flashing, persistent) and has no "pause" state.

## 2. Social Acceptance

**Core drive:** The desire to belong, be approved of, gain status, and avoid social rejection.

**What it looks like in design:**
- Like, reaction, and share counts
- Follower and friend counts
- Social proof indicators ("12 of your friends are using this")
- Public profiles and activity feeds
- FOMO indicators ("Trending," "Everyone is talking about this")
- Referral mechanics tied to social relationships

**When it supports users:**
- Social features connect people who genuinely want to connect.
- Sharing is optional and the user controls their visibility.
- Social proof is accurate and helps users make informed decisions.

**When it becomes manipulative:**
- The product manufactures social pressure by making participation public by default and privacy a buried setting.
- FOMO is used to drive engagement rather than inform. "You're missing out" is the default emotional register.
- Social metrics (likes, followers) are made prominent to create anxiety about social standing.
- Referral mechanics turn users into recruiters who leverage personal relationships for platform growth.

**Examples:**
- 🟢 A book club app where users optionally share reading lists with friends they choose.
- 🟡 A social platform that shows "Suggested for you" based on what friends liked, creating pressure to conform.
- 🔴 An app that posts a user's activity to their social feed without explicit per-action consent and makes opting out require navigating three settings screens.

## 3. Fear

**Core drive:** The desire to avoid loss, danger, negative outcomes, or being left behind.

**What it looks like in design:**
- Countdown timers and limited-time offers
- Warning messages and alert notifications
- Loss aversion framing ("Don't lose your streak!", "Your account will be deleted")
- Threat language in onboarding ("If you don't set this up, you won't be protected")
- Scarcity indicators ("Only 2 left!", "Selling out fast")
- Fear-of-missing-out (FOMO) as a primary engagement driver

**When it supports users:**
- Warnings protect users from genuine harm (security alerts, health reminders).
- Deadlines are real and the user benefits from knowing about them.
- The user can act on the information without additional commitment.

**When it becomes manipulative:**
- Countdowns are artificial — they reset or the offer is always available. The urgency is manufactured.
- Fear is used to drive actions that primarily benefit the platform (data sharing, upselling, engagement) rather than the user.
- Loss aversion is weaponized so that inaction feels more painful than action, even when action is not in the user's interest.
- The product creates the fear and then sells the solution.

**Examples:**
- 🟢 A password manager that warns users about reused passwords and offers a one-click fix.
- 🟡 An e-commerce site that shows "Only 1 left at this price!" when stock is actually unlimited.
- 🔴 A security app that displays fake threat warnings to upsell premium subscriptions.

## 4. Power

**Core drive:** The desire for control, influence, dominance, and the ability to affect outcomes.

**What it looks like in design:**
- Leaderboards and competitive rankings
- Admin roles and permission hierarchies
- Exclusive access and VIP tiers
- Competitive features (vs. mode, challenges, tournaments)
- Status indicators (verified badges, elite labels, premium markers)
- Features that let users control or influence others (moderation tools, voting)

**When it supports users:**
- Competition is consensual and both parties benefit from participation.
- Power features are used for legitimate coordination (moderation, organization, governance).
- Users can opt out of competitive contexts without losing core functionality.

**When it becomes manipulative:**
- Leaderboards are inescapable — the user is ranked whether they want to be or not.
- Power features create haves and have-nots within the product, driving spending or engagement to climb the hierarchy.
- Competitive mechanics exploit users who feel their status is threatened.
- Admin or moderator roles concentrate power in ways that are not accountable.

**Examples:**
- 🟢 A project management tool where team leads can assign tasks with transparent permissions.
- 🟡 A learning platform that publicly ranks all students by default, with no option to hide your rank.
- 🔴 A freemium game where paying users get weapons that make them dominant over non-paying users in competitive modes.

## 5. Incentive

**Core drive:** The desire for extrinsic reward — tangible gain that comes from outside the activity itself.

**What it looks like in design:**
- Discounts, coupons, and promotional pricing
- Points systems and redeemable credits
- Cashback and rebate offers
- Referral bonuses ("Give $10, Get $10")
- Free trials with auto-enrollment
- Loyalty programs and tiered rewards

**When it supports users:**
- The incentive is transparent — the user understands what they are giving in exchange (data, attention, money).
- The reward genuinely aligns with the user's goals.
- The user can take the incentive without hidden strings (auto-renewal, data harvesting, lock-in).

**When it becomes manipulative:**
- The incentive obscures the true cost. "Free" requires data sharing, auto-enrollment, or attention commitment that is not clearly disclosed.
- Rewards shift intrinsic motivation to extrinsic (the overjustification effect). The user who once enjoyed the activity now only does it for points.
- Loyalty programs are designed to be just complex enough that users cannot easily calculate whether they are getting value.
- Referral incentives turn users into salespeople who may pressure friends and family.

**Examples:**
- 🟢 A grocery app that offers genuine discounts with clear terms and no data requirement beyond what is needed for the transaction.
- 🟡 A ride-sharing app that offers "bonus" rides to drivers only if they accept a minimum number of rides during peak hours, pushing them to work when they would rather rest.
- 🔴 A financial app that gives new users a cash bonus for signing up and linking their bank account, but buries the auto-subscription to a paid tier in the terms.
