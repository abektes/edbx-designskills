# CIDER — The Five Stages: Detailed Reference

This document provides full stage definitions, facilitation notes, and timing guidance for each stage of the CIDER method.

---

## Stage C — Critique

### What Happens

Designers examine a design artifact and systematically surface assumptions it makes about users. Each assumption is stated in the format "This design assumes the user [X]" and tagged with one of four categories: Ability, Capacity, Environment, Resources.

### Why It Matters

Assumptions are invisible by definition. The people who embedded them in a design usually cannot see them — they feel like "how the world works" rather than "what I decided to believe." Critique makes the invisible visible. Without this stage, the rest of CIDER has nothing to work with.

### Facilitation Notes

**Opening prompt:** "Look at this design. For a moment, imagine the person it was designed for. What can they do? What do they know? Where are they? What do they have? Now ask: what if that isn't true?"

**Encourage breadth across categories.** Most designers default to Ability assumptions first (vision, hearing, motor control). Prompt deliberately for Environment and Resources — these are often where the most surprising assumptions hide.

**Do not evaluate assumptions yet.** Stage C is about surfacing, not judging. List everything, even assumptions that seem reasonable. The surprising ones are often the most valuable, but you won't know which those are until you see the full list.

**Push for specificity.** "This design assumes the user can see" is too vague. "This design assumes the user can distinguish red from green in a 12px icon at arm's length" is specific enough to imagine exclusion.

**Solo mode:** A solo designer should aim for 5–10 assumptions. Use all four category prompts systematically.

**Team mode:** Each participant generates their own list independently (5 minutes), then shares. The group list is almost always longer than any individual's because different people notice different things.

### Timing

- Solo: 10–15 minutes
- Team: 15–20 minutes (5 min individual + 10–15 min sharing)

### Output

A bullet list of 5–10 named assumptions, each tagged with its category.

## Stage I — Imagine

### What Happens

The designer selects one assumption from Stage C and constructs a vivid, specific scenario showing how that assumption leads to a real person being excluded.

### Why It Matters

Abstract awareness of exclusion ("some users might be left out") does not drive design change. Vivid, specific, humanized scenarios do. When a designer can see a named person struggle with a named task in a named place, the motivation to redesign becomes personal and concrete.

### Facilitation Notes

**Name a person.** Not "a wheelchair user" but "Tomás, who uses a power chair and navigates a campus with broken sidewalk panels near the science building." Specificity forces the designer to think through real constraints rather than stereotyped ones.

**Describe the moment.** What is the person trying to do? What goes wrong? How does it feel? The scenario should be narrative, not analytical. Write it like you're watching it happen.

**Avoid generic scenarios.** "A user with low vision might have trouble" is not an exclusion scenario. "Keiko, who has macular degeneration, opens the booking form on her phone. The date picker uses a calendar grid with no keyboard alternative. She cannot see which date is selected. She tries three times, each time accidentally booking the wrong week. She gives up and calls the clinic, but the phone line has a 40-minute hold." — that is an exclusion scenario.

**Do not solve yet.** The temptation is to immediately suggest a fix. Resist it. Stay in the problem long enough to feel it.

### Timing

- Solo: 10–15 minutes
- Team: 10–15 minutes (individual writing, then share-out)

### Output

A narrative paragraph (100–200 words) naming a specific person and describing their concrete experience of exclusion.

## Stage D — Design

### What Happens

The designer brainstorms 3–5 redesign proposals that remove or reduce reliance on the assumption identified in Stage I.

### Why It Matters

CIDER is not just a diagnostic — it is generative. The value of finding an exclusionary assumption is realized only when something different is designed because of it. This stage turns critique into action.

### Facilitation Notes

**Encourage range.** The first redesign idea is usually the most obvious (and often the most incremental). Prompt for variety: "What's a low-tech version? What would a systemic change look like? What if you redesigned the whole service model instead of just the interface?"

**No feasibility filtering yet.** This is divergent brainstorming. Wild ideas are welcome. Feasibility, cost, and implementation come later. The goal is to expand the solution space, not to pick a winner.

**Label approach types.** For each proposal, note whether it is low-tech, high-tech, systemic, or interaction-level. This helps the designer see the breadth of the solution space.

**Build on the scenario, not just the assumption.** The best redesigns address the specific moment of exclusion described in Stage I, not just the abstract category. "Add alt text" is an abstract solution. "When Keiko focuses the date field, announce the currently selected date aloud and let her type a date in plain text" is grounded in her scenario.

### Timing

- Solo: 10–15 minutes
- Team: 15–20 minutes (individual brainstorm + share-out + group discussion)

### Output

A numbered list of 3–5 redesign proposals, each briefly described with its approach type noted.

## Stage E — Expand

### What Happens

In team sessions, all assumptions surfaced by individual participants are pooled, deduplicated, clustered, tagged, and reviewed for surprises.

### Why It Matters

Different people carry different assumptions and therefore notice different ones. A shared assumption register captures the collective intelligence of the team and becomes a living artifact the team can reference throughout the project.

### Facilitation Notes

**Pool first, discuss second.** Have each participant share their full list before opening discussion. This prevents anchoring on the first few assumptions shared.

**Deduplicate gently.** Merge assumptions that are truly the same, but keep distinct ones separate even if they are related. "Assumes the user can hear" and "Assumes the user can distinguish between two similar audio tones" are related but not identical — they lead to different exclusion scenarios.

**Flag surprises honestly.** Ask: "Which of these assumptions did you not expect to see?" The surprising ones are often the most valuable because they reveal blind spots the team shares.

**Build the register collaboratively.** Use a shared document, whiteboard, or sticky notes. The register should be visible to everyone throughout the session.

**Solo mode note:** Solo designers can skip this stage or use it as a self-review — re-read the assumption list after a break and flag which ones feel most surprising or most exclusionary.

### Timing

- Team: 15–25 minutes
- Solo: 5–10 minutes (self-review)

### Output

A structured assumption register with columns for number, assumption text, category, surprising (yes/no), and source (which participant surfaced it).

---

## Stage R — Repeat

### What Happens

The designer selects a different assumption from the Stage E register (or Stage C list, if solo) and runs a second full I → D cycle.

### Why It Matters

The first assumption a designer picks is usually the most obvious one — the one they already halfway knew about. The second assumption is often more revealing because it comes from deeper reflection or from another team member's perspective. Repeating the cycle doubles the learning and ensures the method produces more than one path to inclusion.

### Facilitation Notes

**Pick a different assumption.** This is non-negotiable. The whole point of Stage R is to go somewhere new.

**Pick a different category if possible.** If Stage I used an Ability assumption, try an Environment or Resources assumption for Stage R. This broadens the designer's view of exclusion.

**Keep the same quality bar.** The second exclusion scenario should be just as vivid and specific as the first. The redesign proposals should be just as diverse.

**In team mode:** Consider having participants swap assumptions — each person imagines exclusion from an assumption they did not surface themselves. This cross-pollination is one of the most powerful features of team CIDER sessions.

### Timing

- Solo: 15–20 minutes (I: 10 min + D: 10 min)
- Team: 20–25 minutes

### Output

A complete I → D cycle: one exclusion scenario and 3–5 redesign proposals, on a different assumption than the first cycle.

---

## Total Session Timing

| Mode | Stages | Time |
|------|--------|------|
| Solo quick | C + I + D | 30–45 minutes |
| Solo full | C + I + D + R | 50–70 minutes |
| Team full | C + I + D + E + R | 75–110 minutes |
| Workshop (multiple designs) | Two full CIDER cycles on different artifacts | 2–3 hours |
