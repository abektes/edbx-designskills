# CIDER — Assumption Categories

The four assumption categories are the analytical backbone of CIDER. Every assumption surfaced in Stage C should be tagged with one of them. Together, they ensure the designer examines exclusion from multiple angles, not just the most obvious ones.

---

## Ability

What the user's body and mind can do right now, in the moment of use.

Ability assumptions are about physical, sensory, cognitive, and emotional capabilities. They are the most commonly recognized category in accessibility work, but CIDER pushes beyond standard accessibility checklists by asking designers to surface *specific* ability assumptions, not just check WCAG guidelines.

### Examples

- "This design assumes the user can distinguish red from green."
- "This design assumes the user can hold a phone steady with one hand."
- "This design assumes the user can read continuous text without losing their place."
- "This design assumes the user can hear spoken instructions in a quiet room."
- "This design assumes the user can process five pieces of information simultaneously."
- "This design assumes the user can perform precise tap gestures on a small target."
- "This design assumes the user can maintain sustained attention for 10 minutes without interruption."
- "This design assumes the user can interpret facial expressions in video calls."
- "This design assumes the user can type at 30+ words per minute."
- "This design assumes the user can see color contrast at 3:1 ratio."

### Why It Matters

Ability varies across every dimension of human life — permanently (a congenital condition), temporarily (a broken wrist), or situationally (holding a baby). Designing for a narrow ability range excludes more people than most designers realize.

---

## Capacity

What the user knows, understands, and can handle cognitively and temporally.

Capacity assumptions are about knowledge, literacy, cognitive load, and time. They are often confused with Ability, but the distinction matters: Capacity is about what the user has learned or has room to process, not what their body or mind can physically do. A person can have full cognitive ability but zero capacity for a specific task because they lack domain knowledge or are already overwhelmed.

### Examples

- "This design assumes the user knows what a hamburger menu icon means."
- "This design assumes the user understands the difference between 'Save' and 'Save As'."
- "This design assumes the user can hold 7 items in working memory while navigating."
- "This design assumes the user has 15 uninterrupted minutes to complete this flow."
- "This design assumes the user knows their Wi-Fi bandwidth in Mbps."
- "This design assumes the user has used a similar product before."
- "This design assumes the user can interpret a scatterplot."
- "This design assumes the user speaks English fluently."
- "This design assumes the user can divide their attention between two simultaneous tasks."
- "This design assumes the user knows what two-factor authentication is and how to set it up."

### Why It Matters

Capacity is the most underestimated exclusion category. Designs that seem simple to their creators are often opaque to first-time users, people under stress, people in a hurry, or people from different cultural or linguistic backgrounds. Every piece of knowledge a design requires is a potential gate.

---

## Environment

Where and under what conditions the user operates.

Environment assumptions are about the physical, social, and technological context of use. They cover connectivity, lighting, noise, physical space, device type, and social setting. A design that works perfectly in a quiet office on a desktop may fail completely on a noisy bus on a phone with a cracked screen.

### Examples

- "This design assumes the user has a stable broadband connection."
- "This design assumes the user is in a well-lit, quiet room."
- "This design assumes the user is using a screen larger than 13 inches."
- "This design assumes the user can play audio without disturbing others."
- "This design assumes the user is indoors."
- "This design assumes the user is alone and can focus without social interruption."
- "This design assumes the user's device has a recent operating system."
- "This design assumes the user is not in motion (walking, riding, driving)."
- "This design assumes the user has reliable GPS signal."
- "This design assumes the user's screen is not in direct sunlight."

### Why It Matters

Designers typically test in their own environment — a well-lit office, a powerful laptop, a fast connection. Most users are not in that environment. Environment assumptions exclude people not because of who they are but because of where they are.

---

## Resources

What the user has access to — money, tools, social support, institutional backing, permissions, time.

Resource assumptions are about what the user can draw on to complete the task. They include financial resources, tool access, social capital, institutional support, and time. Resource exclusion is often the most systemic form of exclusion because it compounds with other categories — a person with limited resources is more likely to also face capacity and environment constraints.

### Examples

- "This design assumes the user can afford a smartphone made in the last 3 years."
- "This design assumes the user has a credit card or bank account."
- "This design assumes the user has someone they can call for tech support."
- "This design assumes the user has a permanent residential address."
- "This design assumes the user can install software on their device."
- "This design assumes the user has a government-issued photo ID."
- "This design assumes the user can afford to spend 20 minutes on this task."
- "This design assumes the user has access to a printer."
- "This design assumes the user has institutional email (not a personal email)."
- "This design assumes the user has reliable childcare during the interaction."

### Why It Matters

Resource assumptions are the most likely to produce systemic exclusion — exclusion that follows socioeconomic lines. They are also the most likely to be invisible to designers who have never experienced resource scarcity. Surfacing resource assumptions often reveals that a design is not just exclusionary but structurally inequitable.

---

## Using the Categories in Practice

During Stage C, systematically prompt across all four categories. Do not let designers stop after Ability — that is the most familiar category and the one most likely to produce assumptions they already knew about. The richest insights often come from Environment and Resources.

Prompt order recommendation for teams:

1. Start with **Ability** (familiar ground, builds confidence)
2. Move to **Environment** (expands the frame from body to context)
3. Then **Capacity** (shifts from physical to cognitive and temporal)
4. End with **Resources** (most systemic, often most surprising)

This order moves from the personal to the structural, which tends to produce the most generative conversations.
