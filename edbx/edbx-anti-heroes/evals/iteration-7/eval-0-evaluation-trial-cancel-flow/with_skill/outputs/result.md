This is a classic "roach motel" pattern—easy to get into, difficult to get out of. While your Growth PM is correct that many SaaS companies use these tactics, "industry standard" is not an ethical justification. Using the **Anti-Heroes** framework, we can move the conversation from "it feels gross" to "here are the specific manipulative roles this design is playing."

### 1. Frame
*   **Design under review:** MossBowl cancellation flow.
*   **User task:** Ending a recurring payment. The user is likely budget-conscious, busy, or perhaps dissatisfied with the service.
*   **Business goal:** Maximize "Retention" and "LTV" (Lifetime Value) by preventing churn at all costs.
*   **Mode:** Evaluation & Ethical Dialogue.

---

### 2. Anti-Hero hits

| Card | What is happening | Where | Why it is anti-user |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Trap-Setter** | The cancel link is buried under "Plan health"—a label that sounds like a status report, not an exit. | Settings → Plan health | It creates asymmetric friction. Signing up took one tap; leaving requires a scavenger hunt. |
| **Empathy Manipulator** | Using "half-empty fridge" imagery and "7-day cooking streak" copy. | Step 1 (Takeover) | It weaponizes loss aversion and guilt. It targets the user’s emotions rather than their rational decision to stop a service. |
| **The Illusionist** | The "Cancel" option is a small, grey, low-contrast link hidden at the bottom of a scrollable page. | Step 2 (Offer) | It uses visual hierarchy to hide the user's intended action while highlighting the business's preferred action (staying). |

---

### 3. Hero counter-moves

| Paired card | Concrete change | Why it restores user value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Empowerer** | Move the cancel link to the main "Subscription" or "Account" page. Label it "Cancel Subscription." | Restores the user’s agency. A confident brand doesn't need to hide the exit. |
| **Life Coach** | Replace the fridge photo with a neutral summary: "You have 3 meals left in your current cycle." | Provides helpful, objective information about what happens next without the guilt trip. |
| **Unveiler** | Present the 30% discount and the "Cancel anyway" option as two buttons of equal visual weight. | Respects the user's intent. If they stay for the discount, it’s a choice, not a result of being unable to find the exit. |

---

### 4. Ethical Dialogue: Handling the "Every SaaS does it" argument

Your PM is using a "Logical Fallacy" (Appeal to Common Practice). Here is how to use the Anti-Hero language to shift the meeting on Thursday:

*   **The Script:** *"I know many competitors use these patterns, but right now we are acting as a **Trap-Setter**. We’re making it intentionally hard for a customer to stop paying us. This might help our short-term churn metrics, but it destroys long-term brand trust. If we act as an **Empowerer** instead, we make the exit graceful—which means they are much more likely to come back to MossBowl in six months when their schedule or budget changes."*
*   **Follow-up Question:** *"If our product is providing value, why do we feel the need to hide the exit? Are we afraid that if we make it easy to leave, everyone will? If so, that's a product value problem, not a UI problem."*

---

### 5. Necessary friction to keep

Not all friction is bad. You should keep:
1.  **The Confirmation Step:** One screen that asks "Confirm Cancellation?" is good—it prevents accidental clicks that would stop their food delivery.
2.  **The Retention Offer:** It is perfectly ethical to offer a discount (the 30% off). The "Anti-Hero" part is *how* it's hidden. Make the offer, but make the "No thanks" just as easy to tap.
3.  **The Feedback Loop:** Asking "Why are you leaving?" with a few checkboxes is helpful for the team, provided it doesn't block the cancellation.
