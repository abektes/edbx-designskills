This is a classic "Roach Motel" pattern, often defended by the industry-standard argument that "friction equals retention." However, using manipulative friction creates **resentment debt**—users might stay for one more month, but they will never return and will likely speak poorly of the brand.

Since your review is on Thursday, here is a structured ethical critique using the **Anti-Heroes** method to help you move the conversation from "vibes" to principled design choices.

### 1. Frame
*   **Design under review:** MossBowl cancellation flow (v1.0).
*   **User task:** Cancel a subscription to stop being charged.
*   **User emotional state:** Likely frustrated, budget-conscious, or simply finished with the service.
*   **Business goal:** Maximize Retention / LTV (Lifetime Value).
*   **Mode:** Evaluation.

---

### 2. Anti-Hero Hits

| Card | What is happening | Where | Why it is anti-user |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **The Obfuscator** | The cancel link is hidden under a euphemism ("Plan health") rather than standard terminology. | Settings Menu | It forces the user to decode internal company jargon to find a basic account utility. |
| **The Empathy Manipulator** | Using a "half-empty fridge" photo and "losing a streak" to trigger guilt/loss aversion. | Step 1 (Takeover) | It weaponizes the user's emotions and past effort to prevent a logical financial decision. |
| **The Trap-Setter** | Making the exit (small, grey, bottom-of-page) significantly harder than the entry. | Step 2 (Offer Screen) | It creates "asymmetric friction," where it's easy to give money but difficult to stop. |
| **The Nickeling-and-Diming** | Hiding the "Cancel" button behind a mandatory scroll past three upsells. | Step 2 (Offer Screen) | It forces the user to "pay" with their attention and time before they are allowed to leave. |

---

### 3. Hero Counter-moves

| Paired Card | Concrete Change | Why it restores user value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **The Unveiler** | Rename the sub-page to "Subscription & Billing" or "Manage Plan." | Clarity reduces cognitive load and builds trust in the brand's honesty. |
| **The Life Coach** | Replace the fridge photo with a neutral summary: "You have 3 credits left. Use them by [Date] or they will expire." | Provides helpful, objective facts that allow the user to make an informed choice. |
| **The Empowerer** | Make the "Cancel anyway" button visually equal to the "Stay" button (Symmetry). | Respects the user's agency. A user who leaves easily is more likely to return later. |
| **The Liberator** | Allow a "One-click skip" for offers. If they say cancel once, don't make them scroll for the exit. | Values the user’s time and reduces the feeling of being "held hostage." |

---

### 4. Ethical Dialogue: How to talk to your PM

Your Growth PM mentioned that "every SaaS does it." Here are three scripts to pivot that conversation during your review:

*   **The "Brand Debt" Script:** *"While other SaaS companies use these patterns, they are building 'resentment debt.' If we make it hard to leave MossBowl, we ensure that when they do leave, they never come back. Can we look at the 'Empowerer' version where we make the exit graceful to keep the door open for a return?"*
*   **The "Dark Pattern" Script:** *"Right now, we are acting as 'The Trap-Setter.' We’ve made the 'Cancel' link a small, grey link at the bottom of a long scroll. This is a documented dark pattern. If our product is valuable, we shouldn't have to hide the exit to keep people."*
*   **The "Symmetry" Script:** *"If it took one tap to subscribe, it should take a similar amount of effort to unsubscribe. 'Plan health' is an 'Obfuscator' move—let’s use 'The Unveiler' approach and call it what it is: Subscription Management."*

---

### 5. Necessary Friction (What to keep)

Not all friction is bad. You should defend keeping these elements to ensure the user doesn't cancel by mistake:

1.  **The Confirmation Step:** A single "Are you sure you want to cancel?" screen is good friction. It prevents accidental clicks.
2.  **The "What you lose" (Facts only):** Telling the user they will lose their current discount rate or that their data will be deleted after 30 days is **The Life Coach** move—it’s helpful information, provided it’s not wrapped in "sad fridge" imagery.
3.  **The Single Offer:** Presenting one (1) alternative (like the 30% off) is acceptable as long as the "No thanks" option is just as easy to tap as the "Accept" option.
