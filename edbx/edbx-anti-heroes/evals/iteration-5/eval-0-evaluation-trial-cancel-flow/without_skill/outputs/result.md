This is a classic "dark pattern" (or "deceptive design") scenario. While your Growth PM is correct that many companies use these tactics, the regulatory and ethical landscape is shifting rapidly. The FTC in the US and various bodies in the EU are increasingly aggressive about "click-to-cancel" requirements.

Here is a structured ethical critique and a set of recommendations for your Thursday review.

---

### Part 1: The Ethical Critique
You can frame these using the three main pillars of **Deceptive Design**: Obfuscation, Obstruction, and Manipulation.

#### 1. Obfuscation (The "Plan Health" Label)
*   **The Issue:** Hiding a cancellation link under a euphemism like "Plan Health" is a transparency failure. Users expect to find cancellation under "Subscription," "Billing," or "Account Settings."
*   **Ethical Violation:** It violates the principle of **Informed Consent**. If a user cannot find the exit, they are being held in a contract against their active will.
*   **Business Risk:** This creates "support debt." Users who can't find the button will flood your CS channels or file credit card chargebacks, which are more expensive than a lost subscriber.

#### 2. Emotional Manipulation (The Fridge & The Streak)
*   **The Issue:** Using a photo of a "half-empty fridge" is a form of **Confirmshaming**. It attempts to trigger a fear of scarcity or personal failure. Similarly, highlighting the "7-day streak" leverages **Loss Aversion** to make the user feel they are losing "work" they’ve put in.
*   **Ethical Violation:** It targets cognitive biases rather than rational decision-making. It treats the user as a psychological subject to be managed, rather than a customer to be respected.

#### 3. Obstruction (The "Roach Motel")
*   **The Issue:** Making the user scroll past three large cards to find a "small grey link" is a classic **Roach Motel** pattern (easy to get in, hard to get out). You are intentionally increasing "interaction cost" to wear down the user's resolve.
*   **Ethical Violation:** This violates **Autonomy**. By making the "No" option visually subordinate and physically distant, you are overriding the user's stated intent.

---

### Part 2: Rebutting the "Everyone does it" Argument
When the PM says "every SaaS does it," here is your counter-argument:

1.  **Regulatory Risk:** The FTC’s proposed "Click-to-Cancel" rule mandates that cancelling a subscription must be at least as easy as signing up for it. If MossBowl’s signup was one click, the cancellation cannot be a multi-screen obstacle course.
2.  **Brand Equity vs. Short-term Retention:** Forcing someone to stay for an extra month through trickery creates a "hostile" brand association. When that user is ready for a meal kit again in six months, they won't come back to MossBowl because they remember the frustration of leaving.
3.  **The "False Active" Metric:** Growth metrics are inflated if they include "zombie subscribers" who want to leave but are stuck. This leads to poor data for the product team.

---

### Part 3: What to Change

**1. Rename the Entry Point**
*   **Change:** Change "Plan health" to "Manage Subscription" or "Cancel Subscription."
*   **Why:** Honesty builds trust.

**2. Neutralize the "Are you sure?" Screen**
*   **Change:** Remove the guilt-tripping imagery. Replace the "half-empty fridge" with a neutral icon or a photo of the food.
*   **Change:** Instead of "You'll lose your streak," use a neutral summary: "If you cancel, your streak will reset and your last box will arrive on [Date]."

**3. Fix the "Save" Offer (The 30% Off)**
*   **Change:** Present the offer and the cancellation side-by-side with **equal visual weight**.
*   **Design Suggestion:** Two cards of equal size. Left: "Stay for 30% off." Right: "Continue to cancel."
*   **Why:** This respects the user’s intelligence. It turns a "barrier" into a "choice."

**4. The "Exit Survey" over "Exit Barrier"**
*   **Change:** Instead of making them scroll past plan cards, ask: "Why are you leaving?" with 4-5 checkboxes.
*   **Why:** This provides the Growth PM with actual actionable data (e.g., "Too expensive," "Too much food") which is more valuable than a forced retention.

---

### Part 4: What is OK to Keep
You don't have to strip the flow of all persuasion. These elements are ethically acceptable:

*   **The Discount Offer:** It is perfectly ethical to offer an incentive to stay. The ethics only break down when you hide the alternative.
*   **The Loss Aversion (The Streak):** It is okay to remind a user of what they are giving up, provided it is a **factual reminder** and not a **moral judgment**. "Your 7-day streak will reset" is a fact. "Don't be a quitter" (implied by the fridge) is a judgment.
*   **The "Pause" Option:** Offering to "Pause for a week" instead of cancelling is a great, ethical way to retain users who are just going on vacation or have a full fridge.

---

### Summary for your Review:
"Our current flow relies on **deceptive design patterns**—specifically obfuscation and obstruction—that are currently under high regulatory scrutiny by the FTC. While these tactics might give us a short-term bump in retention, they damage our brand's long-term 'LTV' (Lifetime Value) by frustrating users on their way out. I propose we move to a **Choice-Based Offboarding** model: clear labeling, a neutral 'Are you sure' screen, and a side-by-side comparison of our retention offer versus the cancellation link."
