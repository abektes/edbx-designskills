# Stanford Ethics Toolkit Analysis: AI System for Social Welfare Benefits Screening

## Executive Summary

This analysis applies the Stanford Ethics Toolkit to evaluate two approaches for deploying an AI system to screen and determine eligibility for social welfare benefits (housing assistance, food support, unemployment). The toolkit guides us through five sequential stages: Future Story (what world are we creating?), Impacts Explorer (who is affected and how?), Ethics Frame (what ethical principles apply?), Ethics Gauge (how severe are the risks?), and Weighing Options (which approach better serves ethical obligations?).

**Options Under Evaluation:**
- **Option A:** AI as primary decision-maker with human appeals process
- **Option B:** AI as decision-support tool only, with case workers making final decisions

---

## 1. FUTURE STORY: Envisioning the World We're Creating

The Future Story tool asks us to imagine the world three to five years after deployment and describe the lived experience of key stakeholders.

### Future World Under Option A (AI as Primary Decision-Maker)

**Year 2029 - Government Agency Perspective**

The agency has deployed the AI system as the primary screening mechanism across all three benefit programs. Processing time for initial eligibility decisions has dropped from 2-3 weeks to 2-3 days. The appeals process handles about 5-8% of cases, and staff have been redeployed from initial screening to case management and appeals review.

**Year 2029 - Applicant Experience (Approved Case)**

Maria applies for housing assistance online. The AI system reviews her income, employment history, and housing situation within 24 hours. She receives automatic approval. She never speaks to a case worker. The decision feels opaque—she doesn't know exactly how the system weighted her circumstances, but she got the help she needed.

**Year 2029 - Applicant Experience (Rejected Case)**

James applies for unemployment benefits after his factory closes. The AI system denies him based on what appears to be insufficient savings and a wage history it categorizes as unstable. He can appeal, but the appeals process requires him to navigate a complex form, and when a human finally reviews his case three months later, his situation has deteriorated significantly. He's lost his apartment. The human reviewer overturns the decision, but the delay caused irreversible harm.

**Year 2029 - Case Worker Experience**

Case workers now spend their time managing appeals rather than conducting initial assessments. They feel they have less relationship-building time with applicants and less ability to apply discretion to unusual situations. Some report higher stress managing the gap between what the system decided and what they believe is just. Turnover in appeals roles has increased.

**Year 2029 - Societal Pattern**

After five years, data emerges showing that the system denies benefits at higher rates to applicants with non-standard employment histories, recent immigrants, and those with disabilities that affect traditional income documentation. The system was trained on historical data, and that historical data reflected historical biases in case worker decisions. No individual decision feels obviously wrong, but the aggregate pattern harms vulnerable populations disproportionately.

---

### Future World Under Option B (AI as Decision-Support Tool)

**Year 2029 - Government Agency Perspective**

The AI system provides recommendations and flags cases for human attention, but all final decisions remain with case workers. Processing time has improved from 2-3 weeks to 1-2 weeks. The system handles routine administrative tasks and surfaces risks the case worker might miss.

**Year 2029 - Applicant Experience (Typical Case)**

Maria applies for housing assistance. A case worker reviews her file within 3-5 days, with AI-generated summaries of her financial situation and risk factors flagged by the system. The case worker approves her application and discusses next steps for housing placement. Maria felt heard.

**Year 2029 - Applicant Experience (Complex Case)**

James applies for unemployment benefits. The AI system flags his case as "non-standard employment history" and recommends resources specific to contract workers. His case worker, Rosa, reviews this alongside her own knowledge. Rosa recognizes that James's situation is common in manufacturing transitions and makes a judgment call that the system wouldn't have made, approving him for extended benefits while he retrains.

**Year 2029 - Case Worker Experience**

Case workers report that the AI system saves them 2-3 hours per day on administrative tasks and data compilation. They use that time for deeper conversations with applicants, building understanding of nuanced situations. They feel the system amplifies their expertise rather than replacing it. Some worry about becoming over-reliant on the system's recommendations.

**Year 2029 - Societal Pattern**

Data shows more even approval rates across demographic groups, though variation still exists. The variation is more often attributable to specific local knowledge and individual circumstances rather than systematic algorithmic bias. When biases do appear, case workers catch and correct them. The system has reduced processing delays without creating a wall between citizens and government.

## 2. IMPACTS EXPLORER: Who Is Affected and How?

The Impacts Explorer identifies stakeholder groups and maps the consequences of each option across multiple dimensions of wellbeing.

### Stakeholder Groups and Impact Dimensions

#### A. People Applying for Benefits

**Economic Wellbeing**
- **Option A:** Faster approvals for eligible applicants (positive). Faster denials that may be unjust for edge cases (negative). Extended hardship during appeals (negative).
- **Option B:** Slightly slower initial decision but more accurate determination; fewer unjust denials (positive). More time investment by case workers allows for understanding of complex situations (positive).

**Dignity and Agency**
- **Option A:** Applicants experience decisions from an inscrutable system; limited ability to present their full story (negative). Appeals process provides recourse but is more formal (mixed).
- **Option B:** Applicants meet with case workers who can understand context; preservation of human dignity in a vulnerable moment (positive). Slower process may prolong uncertainty (negative).

**Procedural Justice**
- **Option A:** Consistent application of criteria (positive). Difficult to explain decisions (negative). Less opportunity for applicant voice (negative).
- **Option B:** Decisions feel more comprehensible and contestable (positive). More susceptible to inconsistency between case workers (negative).

**Trust in Institutions**
- **Option A:** May increase if outcomes improve; may decrease if system is perceived as unfair or unaccountable (mixed).
- **Option B:** Personal relationship with case worker may increase trust; transparency about reasoning increases legitimacy (positive).

#### B. Case Workers

**Job Security and Meaning**
- **Option A:** Role shifts to appeals and complex cases; potential for job loss if not redeployed well (negative). May lose sense of having made a difference in initial screening (negative).
- **Option B:** Role is enhanced but not threatened; AI removes tedious data entry, freeing capacity for meaningful work (positive).

**Professional Autonomy**
- **Option A:** Reduced autonomy in initial decisions; autonomy concentrated in appeals (negative). May feel overridden by the system.
- **Option B:** Preserved autonomy; enhanced with better information (positive). System serves as a tool rather than a replacement (positive).

**Workload and Stress**
- **Option A:** Initial stress from role change; potential stress from managing appeals queue (mixed).
- **Option B:** Reduced administrative burden; maintains complexity of case work; potential stress from over-reliance on system (mixed).

#### C. Government Agency/Public Administration

**Operational Efficiency**
- **Option A:** Significant reduction in processing time; reduced operational costs; faster benefit distribution (positive).
- **Option B:** Moderate improvement in efficiency; maintains some overhead; balanced with quality improvements (mixed).

**Financial Cost**
- **Option A:** Lower staffing costs; potential increase in litigation/appeals costs if system decisions are frequently contested (mixed).
- **Option B:** Moderate staffing cost reduction; higher quality decisions may reduce litigation and future adjustment claims (positive).

**Legal and Reputational Risk**
- **Option A:** High risk of legal challenges if system produces disparate impacts; reputational risk if errors harm vulnerable people (negative). Potential for efficiency gains to be offset by legal costs (negative).
- **Option B:** Lower legal risk due to human oversight; more defensible decisions (positive). Reputational benefit from human-centered approach (positive).

**Accountability**
- **Option A:** Difficult to establish clear accountability when system causes harm; agency must accept responsibility but cannot easily explain or correct (negative).
- **Option B:** Clear chain of accountability through case workers; easier to investigate and correct errors (positive).

#### D. Vulnerable Populations (Low-income, immigrants, disabled, elderly, etc.)

**Access to Benefits**
- **Option A:** Faster processing may help if they know about benefits; if system is biased, systematic exclusion (mixed/negative).
- **Option B:** Human discretion can catch barriers to access that affect vulnerable groups (positive).

**Protection from Harm**
- **Option A:** Vulnerable people less likely to have strong appeals (or resources to appeal); errors are more likely to cause lasting damage (negative).
- **Option B:** Case workers more likely to identify vulnerability and provide support or exceptions (positive).

**Equity**
- **Option A:** Historical data bias will likely replicate itself; aggregate disparities likely (negative).
- **Option B:** Individual assessment allows for correction of systematic unfairness; requires case worker awareness (mixed/positive).

#### E. Society at Large

**Social Cohesion**
- **Option A:** If perceived as unjust, undermines trust in public institutions; if efficient and fair, strengthens confidence (mixed).
- **Option B:** Human face on government benefits system; preserves perception of fairness (positive).

**Pandemic Resilience**
- **Option A:** System can continue functioning even if staffing is reduced; may be fragile if algorithm fails (mixed).
- **Option B:** Requires sustained staffing; more resilient to technical failures (positive).

**Democratic Participation**
- **Option A:** Citizens feel evaluated by an algorithm rather than represented by government; potentially alienating (negative).
- **Option B:** Citizens interact with public servants; maintains participation in democratic process (positive).

## 3. ETHICS FRAME: What Ethical Principles Apply?

The Ethics Frame identifies the ethical principles most relevant to the decision and articulates stakeholder values.

### Applicable Ethical Principles

#### 1. **Dignity and Respect**
- **Core Principle:** All people deserve to be treated as ends in themselves, not merely as cases to be processed.
- **Application to Benefits Screening:** Applicants are in vulnerable circumstances. Their dignity requires that their situation be understood as a whole person, not reduced to algorithmic variables.
- **Option A Tension:** Dignity may be compromised if the system treats applicants as data points rather than people deserving explanation and respect.
- **Option B Alignment:** Preserved through human interaction and individualized consideration.

#### 2. **Fairness and Justice**
- **Core Principle:** Similar situations should be treated similarly, and decisions should not perpetuate historical injustices.
- **Application to Benefits Screening:** Fairness requires that eligible people are approved and ineligible people are denied based on neutral, legitimate criteria. Historical discrimination in case worker decisions created bias in training data.
- **Option A Risk:** If the system inherits historical bias, it may systematize injustice while appearing objective.
- **Option B Benefit:** Human oversight and awareness of bias patterns can mitigate systematic fairness violations.

#### 3. **Accountability and Transparency**
- **Core Principle:** Decisions that affect people's lives should be explainable and contestable.
- **Application to Benefits Screening:** Applicants have a right to understand why they were denied and to challenge the decision.
- **Option A Challenge:** Algorithmic opacity makes accountability difficult. Even well-intentioned, the system is a "black box" to applicants and potentially to administrators.
- **Option B Support:** Case worker decisions are human-scale and can be explained. Accountability is clearer.

#### 4. **Human Agency and Autonomy**
- **Core Principle:** People should retain control over decisions that affect their lives, including the right to be heard.
- **Application to Benefits Screening:** Applicants have a stake in the decision and deserve agency in the process.
- **Option A Limitation:** Applicants have limited ability to present their case or influence the initial decision.
- **Option B Support:** Case workers ensure applicants are heard and understood.

#### 5. **Beneficence (Doing Good)**
- **Core Principle:** Systems should aim to improve people's welfare and reduce suffering.
- **Application to Benefits Screening:** The goal is to get help to people who need it quickly and accurately.
- **Option A Potential:** Speed of decision-making can reduce immediate suffering for eligible applicants.
- **Option B Potential:** Accuracy and reduced wrongful denials prevent causing additional suffering through errors.

#### 6. **Non-Maleficence (Preventing Harm)**
- **Core Principle:** Systems should avoid causing harm, especially to vulnerable people.
- **Application to Benefits Screening:** Wrongful denial of housing, food, or unemployment assistance directly causes harm. The system should minimize this risk.
- **Option A Risk:** Speed may increase error rates; limited human oversight may miss harmful patterns.
- **Option B Benefit:** Human review acts as a harm-reduction mechanism.

#### 7. **Procedural Justice**
- **Core Principle:** The process by which decisions are made should be perceived as fair, even when people disagree with outcomes.
- **Application to Benefits Screening:** Applicants should feel the process was fair even if denied.
- **Option A Challenge:** Algorithmic decision-making may be perceived as unfair or arbitrary.
- **Option B Support:** Human involvement increases perceived fairness.

#### 8. **Capability and Social Support**
- **Core Principle:** Society should support people in developing capabilities and should address structural inequalities.
- **Application to Benefits Screening:** Benefits exist to protect people in a vulnerable state. The system should enable people to meet basic needs and build back.
- **Option A Question:** If the system systematically denies benefits to certain populations, it may undermine capability development.
- **Option B Support:** Personalized case worker judgment can support capability development.

## 4. ETHICS GAUGE: How Severe Are the Risks?

The Ethics Gauge assesses the severity, likelihood, and reversibility of potential harms.

### Risk Matrix: Option A (AI as Primary Decision-Maker)

#### Risk 1: Algorithmic Bias and Disparate Impact
- **Description:** The system replicates or amplifies historical biases against marginalized groups.
- **Severity:** VERY HIGH - Direct harm to vulnerable populations, systematic exclusion from needed benefits.
- **Likelihood:** HIGH - Training data likely contains historical bias; fairness is notoriously difficult to guarantee in ML systems.
- **Reversibility:** LOW - By the time bias is detected, thousands of people may have been wrongfully denied. Correcting decisions retroactively is resource-intensive.
- **Affected Population:** Low-income people, immigrants, people with disabilities, elderly, minorities.
- **Overall Risk Level:** CRITICAL

#### Risk 2: Opaque Decision-Making and Loss of Accountability
- **Description:** Applicants and case workers cannot understand why decisions were made; accountability is diffused.
- **Severity:** HIGH - Undermines rule of law and due process; prevents correction of errors.
- **Likelihood:** HIGH - Most ML systems lack explainability at the individual decision level.
- **Reversibility:** MEDIUM - Decisions can be appealed, but the original lack of transparency cannot be undone.
- **Overall Risk Level:** CRITICAL

#### Risk 3: Systematic Errors and Edge Cases
- **Description:** The system makes consistent errors in cases that deviate from training data (e.g., self-employed workers, recent immigrants, gig economy workers).
- **Severity:** HIGH - Concentrated harm to people in non-traditional circumstances.
- **Likelihood:** HIGH - Edge cases are inherent to ML systems.
- **Reversibility:** MEDIUM - Can be caught in appeals, but delay causes harm.
- **Overall Risk Level:** CRITICAL

#### Risk 4: Erosion of Due Process
- **Description:** Applicants have limited opportunity to present their case; appeals process is formal and intimidating.
- **Severity:** MEDIUM-HIGH - Affects fundamental fairness; particularly harmful to people with limited literacy or language skills.
- **Likelihood:** HIGH - System design encourages speed over individual consideration.
- **Reversibility:** MEDIUM - Appeals are available but underutilized.
- **Overall Risk Level:** HIGH

#### Risk 5: Speed Creates Brittleness
- **Description:** The system prioritizes fast decisions over accuracy; this creates brittle error patterns.
- **Severity:** MEDIUM - Errors may be correctable but harm is immediate.
- **Likelihood:** MEDIUM - Depends on system design and validation.
- **Reversibility:** MEDIUM - Errors can be caught and corrected.
- **Overall Risk Level:** MEDIUM-HIGH

#### Risk 6: Job Loss and Loss of Institutional Knowledge
- **Description:** Case workers are laid off or reassigned; institutional knowledge about local conditions and vulnerable populations is lost.
- **Severity:** MEDIUM - Affects workers' wellbeing; reduces institutional capacity.
- **Likelihood:** MEDIUM - Depends on agency's commitment to redeployment.
- **Reversibility:** LOW - Once knowledge is lost, it's hard to recoup.
- **Overall Risk Level:** MEDIUM

---

### Risk Matrix: Option B (AI as Decision-Support Tool)

#### Risk 1: Over-Reliance on AI Recommendations
- **Description:** Case workers follow AI recommendations without critical review, ending up with similar bias/error patterns as Option A.
- **Severity:** MEDIUM-HIGH - Defeats the purpose of human oversight.
- **Likelihood:** MEDIUM - Over-reliance is common in decision-support systems; depends on training.
- **Reversibility:** MEDIUM - Can be corrected through better training and system design.
- **Overall Risk Level:** MEDIUM-HIGH

#### Risk 2: Slower Decision-Making
- **Description:** By maintaining human involvement, processing times are slower, delaying help to people in urgent need.
- **Severity:** MEDIUM - Delays harm; less severe than wrongful denial but still real.
- **Likelihood:** MEDIUM-HIGH - Human review takes time.
- **Reversibility:** HIGH - Can be mitigated through process improvements and staffing.
- **Overall Risk Level:** MEDIUM

#### Risk 3: Inconsistency Between Case Workers
- **Description:** Without an algorithm as standard, decisions vary more between case workers; this could be perceived as unfair.
- **Severity:** MEDIUM - Variation can be appropriate (reflecting local knowledge) or inappropriate (reflecting bias). Hard to distinguish.
- **Likelihood:** MEDIUM-HIGH - Human decision-making is inherently variable.
- **Reversibility:** MEDIUM - Can be addressed through training and oversight.
- **Overall Risk Level:** MEDIUM

#### Risk 4: Case Worker Burnout
- **Description:** Even with AI support, case workers carry the emotional and cognitive burden of difficult decisions.
- **Severity:** LOW-MEDIUM - Affects worker wellbeing; can impact service quality if burnout is high.
- **Likelihood:** MEDIUM - Case work is inherently demanding.
- **Reversibility:** HIGH - Can be mitigated through workload management and support.
- **Overall Risk Level:** LOW-MEDIUM

#### Risk 5: System Failure Requires Human Fallback
- **Description:** If the AI system fails, there is no automation to fall back on; processing may slow significantly.
- **Severity:** LOW-MEDIUM - Operational issue rather than ethical issue.
- **Likelihood:** MEDIUM - Systems do fail.
- **Reversibility:** HIGH - Can be planned for with redundancy and manual processes.
- **Overall Risk Level:** LOW-MEDIUM

---

### Comparative Risk Summary

**Option A Risks:** All critical risks center on the introduction of opaque, potentially biased automation at scale with limited human oversight. The agency trades human judgment for speed, accepting high ethical risks.

**Option B Risks:** Risks are primarily about maintaining human effectiveness and consistency. They are more manageable through organizational practices (training, staffing, oversight).

**Verdict on Risk Severity:** Option A presents substantially higher ethical risk, particularly for vulnerable populations. Option B's risks are significant but more manageable and less likely to cause systematic harm.

### Qualitative Arguments for Option B

#### 1. **Precautionary Principle**
When introducing new technology into high-stakes decisions affecting vulnerable populations, the burden of proof is on the technology to demonstrate safety and fairness. Current AI systems cannot reliably guarantee freedom from bias at the individual decision level. The precautionary principle suggests we should err on the side of preserving human judgment.

#### 2. **Rule of Law**
Benefits eligibility is a legal right. Applicants have a right to know why they were denied and to contest the decision. Algorithmic opacity undermines the rule of law. Human decision-making, while imperfect, is consistent with legal due process.

#### 3. **Democratic Governance**
Government agencies derive legitimacy from democratic accountability. When decisions are made by algorithms, the chain of accountability is broken. Preserving human case workers maintains the connection between government action and democratic oversight.

#### 4. **Social Trust**
Research on trust in institutions shows that procedural fairness (whether people feel heard and respected) matters as much as outcomes. Option B preserves procedural fairness. Option A sacrifices it for efficiency.

#### 5. **Error Tolerance**
Benefits administration deals with real human suffering. The cost of false negatives (wrongful denials) far exceeds the cost of false positives (incorrectly approved benefits). A system that prioritizes speed will inevitably increase false negatives. A system that prioritizes human judgment can balance speed with accuracy.

#### 6. **Sustainable Institutions**
Rapid automation without attention to displaced workers and erosion of institutional knowledge undermines the sustainability of the institution itself. Case workers who feel replaced rather than supported are more likely to disengage. Option B maintains institutional stability.

### Conditions Under Which Option A Might Be Defensible

If the agency were to pursue Option A, it would need to satisfy all of the following conditions:

1. **Rigorous Fairness Testing:** Independent, ongoing audits showing zero disparate impact across protected classes and across employment types, income documentation methods, etc. This is extremely difficult to achieve in practice.

2. **Explainability:** Individual decisions must be explainable to applicants at a level they can understand and contest. This requires either interpretable models (which sacrifice accuracy) or robust post-hoc explanation methods.

3. **Robust Appeals:** The appeals process must be genuinely accessible, not just formally available. This requires legal support, clear communication, and short timelines. This would be expensive and would largely negate efficiency gains.

4. **Continuous Monitoring:** Real-time monitoring for bias and errors, with immediate retraining cycles. This requires significant ongoing investment.

5. **Harm Mitigation:** A fund to compensate people wrongfully harmed by the system. This acknowledges the risks are real.

6. **Human Dignity:** The system must be designed with explicit protections for human dignity, including opportunities for applicants to present their full story and case workers to inject judgment.

Given these conditions, Option A would look very similar to Option B (retaining significant human involvement), undermining the efficiency argument for deployment.

---

### Recommendation: Proceed with Option B, with the Following Enhancements

#### Implementation Strategy

1. **AI as Augmentation, Not Replacement**
   - Deploy the AI system to handle administrative tasks: document verification, data compilation, identification of potential eligibility issues.
   - Case workers use AI-generated summaries and recommendations, but make all eligibility decisions themselves.
   - System learns from case worker decisions to improve recommendations over time.

2. **Explicit Anti-Bias Oversight**
   - Implement monthly audits of approval rates by demographic group.
   - If disparities emerge, trigger review and retraining.
   - Maintain historical data on all decisions to identify patterns case workers may miss.

3. **Preserve Human Connection**
   - Maintain case worker interviews for all applicants (or at least for those with complex circumstances).
   - Case workers are trained in trauma-informed approaches; they are equipped to provide support beyond eligibility determination.
   - Build time for relationship and understanding into the process, not just data extraction.

4. **Efficiency Improvements Without Automation Risks**
   - Use AI to streamline document processing and preliminary screening (flagging obvious eligibilities).
   - Implement better tracking systems to reduce case worker time on status updates.
   - Create clear decision trees for routine cases, freeing case workers to focus on complex ones.
   - These can improve processing time without sacrificing human judgment.

5. **Transparent System Design**
   - Case workers and applicants can see what data the system used and what it flagged.
   - All decisions are documented with case worker reasoning.
   - Applicants receive clear, accessible explanations of why they were denied.

6. **Workforce Support**
   - Ensure case workers are redeployed, not laid off, as automation handles some routine tasks.
   - Invest in training case workers to use AI tools effectively.
   - Recognize that case work becomes more sophisticated (less data entry, more judgment), not less important.

7. **Pilot and Iterate**
   - Pilot the system in one region before full deployment.
   - Use the pilot to identify unintended consequences and refine the approach.
   - Build in regular review points to assess whether the system is meeting its goals.

---

### Why Option B Aligns with Ethical Obligations

1. **Respects Dignity:** Applicants are treated as whole people, not algorithmic variables.

2. **Promotes Fairness:** Human judgment can catch and correct bias that algorithms embed.

3. **Maintains Accountability:** Decisions are explainable and contestable.

4. **Preserves Agency:** Applicants and case workers retain agency in the decision.

5. **Reduces Harm:** Human oversight acts as a safety valve, preventing systematic harm.

6. **Sustains Institutions:** Preserves institutional knowledge and workforce morale.

7. **Maintains Democratic Legitimacy:** Government remains accountable to the people.

8. **Enables Capability Development:** Case workers can support people in rebuilding their lives, not just screen for eligibility.

---

## Conclusion

The Stanford Ethics Toolkit analysis reveals that while Option A (AI as primary decision-maker) offers efficiency gains, it introduces unacceptable ethical risks, particularly to vulnerable populations. The tool of speed is purchased at the cost of fairness, accountability, dignity, and the rule of law.

Option B (AI as decision-support tool) balances the legitimate goal of improving processing efficiency with the ethical imperative to treat applicants justly and with dignity. It preserves human judgment where it matters most—in decisions that affect people's access to basic needs.

**Final Recommendation:** Implement Option B with the enhancements described above. This approach serves the government's goal of improving benefits administration while honoring the ethical principles that legitimate government action.

The fundamental principle is this: In decisions affecting access to basic human needs, the burden of proof is on algorithms to demonstrate they improve fairness and accountability. Current AI systems cannot meet this burden. Until they can, human judgment should remain central to the process.
