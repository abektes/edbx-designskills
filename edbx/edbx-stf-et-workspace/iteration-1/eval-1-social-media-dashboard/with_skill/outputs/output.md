## Tool 3 — Ethics Frame

*A practical framework to think through the (potential) benefits and harms of your actions and the possible actions in response*

### Section 1 — ACTION / CREATION

**Social Media Performance Dashboard:** A feature that displays to users comparative engagement metrics showing:
- Their post engagement compared to their own historical average
- Their post engagement compared to similar users (peer benchmarking)
- Visualization and trend tracking of their reach and performance metrics
- Designed to help creators understand their reach and optimize engagement

---

### Section 2 — VALUES (Part One: Explore the values that matter)

**Values driving the work:**
- **Well-being** (flourishing through informed decision-making, growth as a creator)
- **Autonomy** (helping users understand and control their creative direction)
- **Responsibility** (transparency about algorithmic reach and performance factors)
- **Trust** (creators believing the platform supports their success)

**Broader values impacted (both reinforced and potentially undermined):**
- **Well-being:** The dashboard aims to inform and empower, but research indicates it may create anxiety, particularly in younger users. Positive side: creators report motivation to improve. Negative side: comparison-driven stress could undermine psychological flourishing.
- **Autonomy:** Users gain information to make choices about content strategy. However, if the dashboard creates psychological pressure toward metric-chasing, autonomy becomes constrained by incentive structures rather than genuine choice.
- **Justice:** The dashboard assumes equal access to algorithmic reach, but unequal algorithmic reach across demographic groups means the same tool presents unequal baselines. Younger users (often with smaller established networks) see themselves against peers, potentially magnifying disparities. Creators in under-served communities may see lower benchmarks, creating a fairness question: are we showing them accurate comparison groups, or are we making visible an unjust algorithmic reality?
- **Dignity:** Comparison metrics can reduce human creative work to quantified performance, which may undermine the dignity of smaller creators or those whose content does not align with algorithmic preferences.
- **Trust:** Transparency about performance could build trust, but if algorithms favor certain groups, the dashboard may expose (and reinforce) unfair treatment, eroding trust among those disadvantaged.
- **Responsibility:** By showing metrics, the platform demonstrates awareness of creator outcomes. However, responsibility also requires acknowledging the platform's role in shaping those outcomes — not presenting algorithmic reach as if it were neutral truth independent of platform design.

**How do actions influence these values?**

- The dashboard **reinforces Well-being and Autonomy** for creators who interpret the data as educational feedback and use it to experiment and refine without stress.
- The dashboard **undermines Well-being** for users (especially younger) experiencing comparison anxiety, creating psychological burden rather than flourishing.
- The dashboard **constrains Autonomy** if it creates implicit pressure to optimize for metrics rather than authentic creative goals.
- The dashboard makes **Justice visible but potentially unjust:** If algorithmic reach is unequal, the dashboard makes that visible, but without addressing the underlying inequality, it can reinforce it by making creators in disadvantaged groups aware they are "behind."
- The dashboard **challenges Trust** if creators learn their reach is partly determined by algorithmic factors outside their control, and trust erodes further if the platform is not transparent about those factors.

---

### Section 3 — BENEFITS

**Preference-based benefits:**
- Creators value transparency and understanding of how their content performs; the dashboard provides this directly.
- Creators want to optimize their presence; the dashboard gives actionable feedback on engagement trends.

**Well-being benefits:**
- **For engaged, confident creators:** Seeing positive trends creates motivation and satisfaction.
- **For growing creators:** Understanding historical progress validates growth and encourages continued effort.
- **For experimental creators:** Comparing performance across different content types helps identify what resonates.

**Extent assessment:**

| Dimension | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Magnitude** | Moderate to high per individual who engages positively with the data. Creators report motivation effects. |
| **Scope** | Benefits accrue primarily to established creators or those with growth trajectory; less benefit (potentially harm) for smaller creators or newer users. Estimated ~40-50% of creators may experience positive motivation; 30-40% may experience anxiety or demotivation. |
| **Likelihood** | High that engaged creators will experience motivation; moderate to high that anxiety will occur in comparison-sensitive users, especially younger demographics. |
| **Duration** | Ongoing if dashboard becomes part of regular creative workflow; likely to intensify over time as creators become more metric-focused. |

**Broader societal benefits:**
- **Transparency value:** The platform demonstrates commitment to showing creators how algorithmic systems affect them, increasing transparency in an opaque ecosystem.
- **Creator agency:** Providing data shifts some power back to creators to understand and respond to their audience.
- **Healthy competition incentive:** Peer benchmarking, if used responsibly, can drive quality improvement and innovation across the creator ecosystem.

### Section 4 — HARMS

**Direct harms (grounded in research concerns):**

1. **Comparison anxiety in younger users:**
   - Younger users report heightened sensitivity to comparison and social comparison bias.
   - Seeing peers with higher engagement may trigger negative self-evaluation and psychological distress.
   - Risk of compounding existing social media-driven anxiety and mental health challenges.
   - **Magnitude:** Moderate to high for affected individuals (anxiety, lowered self-esteem, reduced posting behavior).
   - **Scope:** Primarily younger users (teens, early 20s) and those predisposed to comparison sensitivity; estimated 25-40% of younger cohort significantly impacted.
   - **Likelihood:** High that some level of comparison anxiety will occur; intensity varies by personality and demographics.
   - **Duration:** Enduring while using the feature; may compound over time if users repeatedly engage with negative comparisons.

2. **Metric-chasing behavior (incentive misalignment):**
   - The dashboard creates salience for metrics, which can shift creator motivation from authentic expression to engagement optimization.
   - "What gets measured gets managed" — creators may alter content strategy to chase metrics rather than serve their authentic creative vision or audience needs.
   - This can degrade content quality across the platform (homogenization toward what algorithms reward rather than diversity of creator voices).
   - **Magnitude:** High — creators fundamentally alter their creative behavior and sense of purpose.
   - **Scope:** Affects a broad swath of creators who adopt metric-optimization as strategy; especially impacts smaller creators who feel pressure to "catch up."
   - **Likelihood:** High. Research on gamification and metrics shows that salient metrics almost always shift behavior. Even well-intentioned creators cannot easily resist this dynamic.
   - **Duration:** Enduring and likely escalating as creators invest more emotional and strategic energy into the metrics.

3. **Unequal algorithmic reach visibility (fairness harm):**
   - The algorithm does not distribute reach equally across demographic groups. Some groups (e.g., certain age ranges, content categories, or geographic regions) receive amplification; others do not.
   - The dashboard makes this algorithmic inequality visible and personal. A creator in a disadvantaged group sees their benchmarks are lower — not because they produce worse content, but because the algorithm favors others.
   - This visibility can demoralize creators who are systemically under-served, reinforcing beliefs that "the platform is unfair to me."
   - It can also paradoxically *legitimize* the inequality by making it visible and normalized ("this is just how the algorithm works").
   - **Magnitude:** High for creators in under-served groups; they internalize algorithmic disadvantage as personal inadequacy.
   - **Scope:** Affects creators in underrepresented demographic groups, smaller or emerging communities, non-mainstream content categories. Estimated 20-40% of creator base, depending on algorithm.
   - **Likelihood:** Certain to occur; the inequality already exists, and the dashboard makes it visible.
   - **Duration:** Enduring; creators will repeatedly encounter their lower benchmarks.

4. **Dignity harm through quantification and comparison:**
   - Human creativity is reduced to a number (engagement metric). For some creators, this quantification undermines the intrinsic dignity of creative work.
   - Comparison framing ("you are below average") can feel dehumanizing, treating the creator as a data point rather than a person with unique value.
   - Smaller creators or those in less-algorithmic-friendly niches may feel their work is being judged as "worth less" because metrics are lower.
   - **Magnitude:** Moderate to high for creators who identify deeply with their creative work; lower for those who view content as purely instrumental.
   - **Scope:** Affects quality-focused creators, niche creators, and those in underrepresented groups; estimated 20-30%.
   - **Likelihood:** Moderate to high, depending on how the dashboard frames metrics (comparative or absolute).
   - **Duration:** Ongoing psychological effect if the dashboard is regularly used.

5. **Feedback loop harm — Algorithmic amplification of biases:**
   - If creators systematically optimize based on the dashboard's metrics, and those metrics reflect algorithmic biases, creators' own content strategies will reinforce those biases.
   - Example: If the algorithm favors a particular aesthetic or demographic, the dashboard encourages creators to move toward that aesthetic, which reinforces the algorithm's preference.
   - Over time, this creates a feedback loop where creator behavior and algorithm behavior lock in, reducing diversity and entrench unfairness.
   - **Magnitude:** Systemic; affects the entire creator ecosystem and platform culture.
   - **Scope:** All creators and users of the platform.
   - **Likelihood:** Very high; this is a known dynamic in metrics-driven systems.
   - **Duration:** Compounding over time.

6. **Potential for unintended misuse and manipulation:**
   - Bad actors may use the dashboard to identify underperforming creators and target them with harassment or manipulation ("your content is below average").
   - The dashboard may be weaponized to discourage certain creators from posting.
   - Influencers or competitors may use benchmarking data to unfairly pressure or copy smaller creators.
   - **Magnitude:** Moderate to high for targeted individuals.
   - **Scope:** Smaller creators, marginalized creators, younger creators (more vulnerable to online abuse).
   - **Likelihood:** Moderate; depends on platform culture and safeguards.
   - **Duration:** Episodic but potentially severe if targeting occurs.

**Key distributional patterns:**

1. **Privilege-concentration pattern:** Established creators with existing reach advantage benefit most; emerging creators with no reach advantage see themselves as "behind" and face demotivation.

2. **Demographic disparity pattern:** Creators in demographic groups favored by the algorithm see fair or positive benchmarks; creators in disfavored groups see themselves as perpetually below average — not because of effort but because of algorithmic bias.

3. **Age-sensitivity pattern:** Younger users (more susceptible to social comparison and anxiety) are exposed to a comparison-heavy interface at an age when social comparison is most psychologically taxing.

4. **Visibility of injustice:** Unlike the current state where algorithmic inequality is invisible, the dashboard makes it visible and personal, potentially increasing awareness of unfairness without addressing its causes.

**Fairness assessment:**

- **Is the distribution fair?** No. The dashboard amplifies existing inequalities by making them visible and salient. Those already advantaged (larger audience, favored demographics) gain further validation and motivation; those already disadvantaged face demotivation and visible confirmation of their disadvantage.

- **Are already-marginalized groups facing outsized harms?** Yes. Creators in underrepresented demographic categories, smaller communities, and non-mainstream niches face the combined harms of: (a) lower algorithmic reach (baseline inequality), (b) visible awareness of lower benchmarks (psychological harm), and (c) potential for targeted harassment or discouragement.

- **Are privileged groups capturing most of the benefit?** Yes. Established, majority-demographic creators with positive engagement trends benefit most from validation and optimization data.

- **Are some unable to access benefits due to barriers?** Yes. Younger creators, those with anxiety or comparison sensitivity, and those facing algorithmic disadvantage cannot psychologically or strategically access the benefits the dashboard intends to offer.

### Section 6 — VALUES (Part Two: Plan for action in alignment with values)

**Values in tension:**

The core tension is between **Autonomy** (enabling creators to understand and control their reach) and **Well-being** (protecting creators from anxiety and demotivation). The feature empowers some users while harming others.

A secondary tension: **Transparency** (showing metrics honestly) and **Justice** (not reinforcing or amplifying unfair algorithmic outcomes). Making injustice visible without addressing it can increase harm.

**Values prioritization in the current design:**

The current design prioritizes:
1. **Autonomy and Transparency** — enabling creator choice and platform honesty
2. Over **Well-being** — psychological safety of vulnerable users
3. Over **Justice** — fairness in reach distribution
4. Over **Dignity** — respect for the intrinsic value of creative work independent of metrics

**What values should take precedence?**

For a responsible launch, the prioritization should shift to:
1. **Well-being** and **Justice** must be foundational constraints. The feature cannot launch in a way that predictably harms vulnerable users or amplifies unfair algorithmic outcomes.
2. **Autonomy** can be enabled, but with guardrails that prevent the feature from creating psychological pressure toward metric-chasing or comparison.
3. **Dignity** requires that metrics be contextualized and that the dashboard does not reduce creative work to a quantified judgment.

---

### Section 7 — HOW TO EXPAND BENEFITS

**Low-hanging fruit:**

1. **Segment the dashboard by audience:** Show different metrics to different creator types. For emerging creators, emphasize absolute growth and personal best (comparison to self, not peers). For established creators, include peer comparison. This reduces inappropriate comparison for vulnerable users while preserving autonomy for those who can handle it.

2. **Contextualize metrics:** Add explanatory text and qualitative feedback alongside quantitative metrics. Example: "Your recent post received 2,500 engagements. For posts in your category, the average is 3,200. Here are 3 content factors that might affect reach: [factors]. Consider experimenting with [suggestions]." This shifts the frame from "you are below average" to "here is what affects reach; you can experiment."

3. **Celebrate progress, not position:** Emphasize trend, growth, and historical comparison over peer ranking. "You've grown 15% in engagement this month" vs. "You're 27th among your peer group." This preserves motivation without comparison anxiety.

4. **Surface algorithmic transparency:** Explicitly explain which algorithmic factors affect reach (category, recency, user engagement patterns, etc.). This helps creators understand that reach is partly a function of algorithmic design, not purely creative quality. Reduces the conflation of "low reach" with "poor content."

5. **Add value-driven framing:** Ask creators about their goals and values upfront. "Do you create to build a tight community, to reach a broad audience, or to experiment with format?" Show metrics aligned with their stated values, not generic metrics. This preserves autonomy and reduces metric-chasing toward irrelevant targets.

6. **Highlight diverse success stories:** Curate and surface examples of creators who succeed with different strategies — niche creators with small but engaged communities, creators who grew slowly, creators who prioritize quality over quantity. This counters the implicit message that "high engagement = success."

---

### Section 8 — HOW TO REDUCE HARMS

**Preventing comparison anxiety:**

1. **Opt-in, not opt-out, for peer comparison:** Peer comparison is the feature most likely to cause anxiety. Make it opt-in. Default to self-comparison and absolute metrics. This respects autonomy (users can choose comparison) while protecting vulnerable users by default.

2. **Age-gating or sensitivity warnings:** For users under 18 or those self-identifying as comparison-sensitive, either restrict peer comparison or add a friction point ("Research shows peer comparison can increase anxiety for some users. Are you sure you want to see this?"). This acknowledges the known harm and gives users agency.

3. **Mental health resources:** Pair the dashboard with optional links to mental health resources (e.g., "Feeling anxious about your metrics? Try these resources"). This signals responsibility and provides support.

**Preventing metric-chasing behavior:**

4. **Separate engagement metrics from algorithmic visibility:** Show creators two distinct numbers: (a) actual audience engagement (likes, comments, shares) and (b) algorithmic factors (reach, impressions). Separate them visually and explain the difference. This helps creators understand that metrics are not purely about content quality.

5. **Introduce counter-metrics:** Alongside engagement metrics, show metrics that reflect other values. Example: "Average watch time per viewer: 4.2 min" (depth of engagement, not breadth). Or "Audience retention: 72%" (loyalty, not viral reach). This creates space for creators to optimize for multiple values, not just engagement count.

6. **Deprioritize novelty in the ranking:** If the dashboard shows peer benchmarks, do not update them more frequently than monthly. Real-time comparison creates urgency and pressure. Monthly updates reduce the psychological pressure to constantly optimize.

7. **Add friction to metric-focused features:** If the dashboard includes suggestions for how to increase engagement, make those suggestions come from creators' own stated goals or from optional "optimization mode," not as default. This preserves the choice not to chase metrics.

**Reducing visibility of algorithmic injustice without hiding it:**

8. **Transparent benchmarking:** If benchmarks exist, explicitly state the comparison group. "Your benchmark peer group: creators in [category] posting [frequency] in [region]. This group has these demographic characteristics: [data]." This makes visible why benchmarks are what they are and acknowledges that benchmarks may reflect algorithmic biases, not universal success standards.

9. **Algorithmic equity dashboard:** Create a separate, optional dashboard showing creators how algorithmic reach varies across demographic groups and content categories on the platform. "Creators posting [content type] receive 40% lower initial reach than [other type]. This is a platform-wide pattern, not a reflection of content quality." This makes injustice visible and signals the platform's awareness, creating space for the platform to commit to remedies.

10. **Equity-focused support program:** Launch a program specifically for creators in underrepresented groups, offering mentorship, content feedback, and algorithmic transparency. Signal to these creators that the platform recognizes systemic disadvantage and is committed to supporting them beyond the dashboard.

**Protecting dignity:**

11. **Humanize the narrative:** Add creator testimonials, behind-the-scenes insights, and story context to the metrics view. "This creator's [low-engagement post] was an experimental piece exploring [theme]. Here's what they learned." This counters the quantification-only frame and reminds users that metrics do not measure creative value.

12. **Qualitative feedback option:** Offer creators the ability to submit work for qualitative feedback (e.g., written critique from experienced creators) alongside quantitative metrics. This preserves space for craft evaluation beyond numbers.

**Preventing misuse and harassment:**

13. **Privacy by default:** Do not make comparative metrics visible to other users (e.g., a creator's followers should not see their low benchmark rank). The metrics are for the creator's own use, not for external judgment.

14. **Moderation and reporting:** Establish clear policies and reporting mechanisms for users who attempt to use the dashboard to harass or discourage creators (e.g., messaging "your engagement is below average"). Actively moderate.

15. **Anti-harassment tooling:** Give creators tools to hide metrics from specific users or communities, or to disable peer comparison entirely if they experience targeted discouragement.

---

### Section 9 — BOTTOM LINE

**What changes in your plans will you make?**

**Required changes to proceed responsibly:**

1. **Peer comparison must be opt-in, not default.** Default dashboard shows self-comparison (historical trends, personal growth) only. Peer benchmarking is available via explicit opt-in and with age/sensitivity warnings for users under 18.

2. **Transparent algorithmic contextualization is non-negotiable.** Every metric must be accompanied by explanation of which algorithmic factors affect it. "Your engagement: 2,500 interactions. This reflects: content category (moderate reach), posting time (high visibility), audience size (smaller reach base). Your effort and quality are separate variables — here's what you control."

3. **Launch a parallel equity program.** Before or concurrent with dashboard launch, establish a targeted support program for creators in algorithmically disadvantaged groups: transparency reports, mentorship, and a commitment to work on algorithmic bias. Make visible that the platform is aware of and actively addressing fairness.

4. **Implement mental health protections by default.** All users under 18 receive a friction point before accessing peer comparison. All users have access to mental health resources paired with the feature. Monitor for anxiety-related outcomes (decreased posting, engagement, user reports) and adjust if harm is observed.

5. **Introduce counter-metrics as standard.** Alongside engagement count, show quality/depth metrics (time watched, audience retention, repeat viewership). This creates space for creators to value outcomes other than pure reach and reduces metric-chasing.

6. **Establish a 6-month evaluation period with clear success metrics.** Measure: (a) comparison anxiety rates in younger users (via survey, community feedback), (b) changes in creator behavior (shift toward metric-chasing vs. authentic creation), (c) engagement with algorithmic transparency (how many creators read the algorithmic explanation), (d) equity outcomes (do creators in underrepresented groups show improved engagement or continued disadvantage). If any metric shows significant harm, pause peer comparison or reduce feature scope.

7. **Do not launch without privacy controls.** Comparative metrics must never be visible to other users. Creators must have control over whether to share their dashboard views. This prevents the feature from becoming a basis for comparison-based harassment.

**Specific actionable next steps:**

- [ ] Conduct research with 20-30 younger creators (ages 13-22) and comparison-sensitive creators to understand anxiety triggers and validate mitigation strategies
- [ ] Partner with mental health experts to design the opt-in friction, resource links, and evaluation metrics for psychological outcomes
- [ ] Audit algorithmic reach distribution across demographic groups and content categories; create transparency report on fairness
- [ ] Design and launch algorithmic equity support program with recruiting, mentorship, and community building for disadvantaged creator groups
- [ ] Build A/B test: default self-comparison vs. default peer comparison, measuring comparison anxiety, metric-chasing behavior, and content diversity across groups
- [ ] Draft privacy policy changes ensuring comparative metrics are private and cannot be used for inter-creator comparison or harassment
- [ ] Establish 6-month evaluation gate with clear decision criteria for scope adjustments or feature pause

---

**Values affirmed in bottom line:**
- **Well-being:** Opt-in comparison and mental health protections defend psychological flourishing, especially for vulnerable users.
- **Justice:** Algorithmic transparency and equity support program signal commitment to addressing unfair reach distribution, not just making it visible.
- **Autonomy:** Users retain agency in what they measure themselves against and how they use the data.
- **Dignity:** Counter-metrics and qualitative feedback preserve space for non-quantified creative value.
- **Responsibility:** Clear evaluation gates and willingness to pause the feature if harm occurs demonstrates accountability.

## Tool 4 — Ethics Gauge

*A systematic assessment across four core ethical dimensions — beneficial, harmful, fair, and empowering*

### Dimension 1 — HOW IS IT BENEFICIAL?

**Spectrum 1: Limited benefit to individuals ←→ Great benefit to individuals**

**Assessment: Neutral to Positive** (with significant variance by user segment)

*Position:* The dashboard provides **moderate to great benefit for established and growth-oriented creators**, but **limited or negative benefit** for emerging, anxious, or algorithmically-disadvantaged creators. Weighted across the creator population, this is a **neutral to slightly positive** net (perhaps 55% of creators experience net benefit, 45% experience net harm or neutral effect).

*Observable indicators if benefit occurs:*
- Creators report increased confidence in their content strategy (qualitative interviews)
- Creators make intentional, informed content decisions (analysis of creator statements about content choices)
- Creators experience satisfaction from seeing their growth reflected in metrics (sentiment analysis, engagement surveys)
- Creator retention and posting frequency increase for benefit-experiencing cohorts (analytics)
- Creators report the feature helped them "understand their audience better" (survey open-ended)

*Observable indicators if benefit is limited:*
- Creators report feeling anxious or inadequate when viewing metrics (community sentiment analysis)
- Creators report changing content strategy in ways they describe as "chasing metrics" not "authentic" (qualitative research)
- Creator posting frequency decreases after viewing dashboard, especially in emerging creator cohorts (analytics)
- Creators report the feature made them "feel judged" or "compared" (community feedback)

*Reflection — Measurement & Investigation:*
- **How do we measure benefit?** Benefit exists on a spectrum: some creators genuinely benefit from transparency, others experience the same data as harmful. We need granular cohort analysis: does benefit differ by age, current audience size, content category, or self-reported comparison sensitivity? 
- **Key research question:** For which creator types is the benefit of transparency outweighed by psychological harm?
- **Investigation needed:** Conduct 4-week behavioral study with three cohorts (established creators, emerging creators, younger/sensitive creators) to measure: posting frequency, content quality (coded by independent raters), self-reported motivation, anxiety levels (validated scale), and creator satisfaction.

---

**Spectrum 2: Benefit limited to few select people ←→ Large numbers and multiple groups benefit**

**Assessment: Negative** (benefit concentrated in privileged segments)

*Position:* Benefit is **concentrated among established creators and those in algorithmic-majority demographics**. Emerging creators, younger users, creators in underrepresented categories, and comparison-sensitive individuals receive limited or negative benefit. **Estimated 30-40% of creators benefit; 40-50% experience net harm or neutral effect; 10-20% uncertain.** Benefit is **not widely distributed** across creator segments.

*Observable indicators:*
- Benefit-receiving cohorts: creators with >10k followers, creators in algorithmic-preferred categories, creators >25 years old, creators without prior anxiety history
- Limited-benefit cohorts: creators with <1k followers, creators in niche/underrepresented categories, creators <25 years old, creators with documented comparison sensitivity

*Reflection — Investigation Needed:*
- **Equity question:** If benefit is concentrated in already-privileged creator segments, does this feature increase or decrease overall platform equity?
- **Research gap:** We don't yet have demographic breakdowns of creator benefit from our user research. Immediate next step: disaggregate feedback by age, audience size, content category, and self-reported mental health to understand which groups truly benefit.
- **Actionable insight:** If benefit concentrates in privileged groups, the feature may inadvertently widen the gap between successful and struggling creators. This argues for either: (a) redesign to expand benefit distribution, or (b) parallel interventions that specifically support under-benefiting groups (e.g., the equity program mentioned in the Ethics Frame bottom line).

---

**Spectrum 3: Action unlikely to succeed / low likelihood of benefit ←→ Very likely the benefit will be achieved**

**Assessment: Positive** (benefit is likely to be realized)

*Position:* For creators who **choose to engage with the feature** and are **not experiencing comparison anxiety**, the likelihood of benefit is **high**. The mechanics are straightforward: showing creators how their engagement changes over time almost certainly creates the opportunity to understand trends and adjust strategy.

However, for the subset experiencing anxiety or metric-chasing compulsion, the "benefit" (understanding) is overwhelmed by the "harm" (anxiety or behavioral distortion), so the likelihood of net positive benefit is **low to neutral**.

**Weighted assessment:** Across all creators, the likelihood of achieving positive net benefit is **moderate** (maybe 50-60% of creators will experience the intended benefit).

*Observable indicators:*
- High-likelihood cohorts report: "I used this data to try a new approach and it worked" (post-interview), increased experimentation in content, strategic content decisions
- Low-likelihood cohorts report: avoidance of the dashboard, increased anxiety when viewing, difficulty sleeping/focus after viewing metrics

*Reflection — What do we need to investigate?*
- **Psychological research question:** What proportion of our creator base has comparison sensitivity sufficient that the dashboard's "benefit" is lost to anxiety? Research suggests 20-40% of young people have elevated social comparison sensitivity. We need a validated scale assessment with our creator cohort.
- **Behavioral change question:** For creators who report positive intention ("I'll optimize my strategy"), how many actually follow through vs. how many experience metric-chasing compulsion? This requires 8-12 week longitudinal tracking.
- **Mechanism question:** What design features increase the likelihood that creators interpret the data as informative feedback vs. as judgment? Test framing variations (e.g., "Here's what changed" vs. "Here's how you compare").

---

**Dimension 1 Summary Reflection:**

The dashboard is **conditionally beneficial** — offering real value to some creator segments and real harm to others. The benefit is not widely distributed and the likelihood of net positive benefit depends heavily on psychological factors and design choices we have not fully controlled yet.

**Key actionable insight:** Before launch, the team should commit to collecting baseline data on these three spectra with actual creators and be willing to pause or redesign if: (a) benefit is concentrated in privileged groups, or (b) likelihood of benefit for younger/vulnerable creators is below 50%.

### Dimension 2 — HOW IS IT HARMFUL?

**Spectrum 1: Great harm to individuals; more harm than alternatives ←→ Limited harm; less than what already exists**

**Assessment: Negative to Neutral** (moderate harm, likely greater than status quo for some segments)

*Position:* The dashboard creates **moderate to great harm for comparison-sensitive users** (especially younger users), and **introduces new harm** (metric-chasing compulsion, visibility of algorithmic injustice) that doesn't currently exist at this scale.

For these segments, the feature introduces **more harm than the status quo** (which at least keeps algorithmic inequality invisible and doesn't directly trigger comparison anxiety via a designed feature).

For other segments (established creators), the harm is **limited or non-existent**.

**Weighted assessment:** ~35-45% of creators experience moderate to great harm. This is **more harm than status quo alternatives** for these segments.

*Observable harm indicators:*
- Younger users report increased anxiety, self-doubt, or negative self-evaluation after using the feature (validated anxiety scale, daily surveys)
- Creators report changing their content in ways they describe as unhelpful or inauthentic (qualitative, content analysis)
- Emerging creators report demotivation or reduced posting (analytics on posting frequency pre-post feature launch)
- Creators in underrepresented groups report feeling "below average" or "unfair" (sentiment analysis, community comments)
- Harassment incidents increase where dashboard metrics are weaponized against creators (incident reports, community moderation data)

*Reflection — What would we expect to observe if harm occurs?*
- **If comparison anxiety harm is real:** Within 2-4 weeks of launch, we should see: (a) increased mentions of "anxiety," "comparison," or "inadequacy" in community discussions; (b) increased use of mental health resources linked from the feature; (c) decreased posting frequency in younger age cohorts; (d) higher bounce rates from the dashboard among younger users (they view it once then avoid it).
- **If metric-chasing harm is real:** Within 4-8 weeks, we should see: (a) creators reporting changes in content strategy explicitly tied to metrics optimization; (b) content diversity metrics decline (more homogeneity toward algorithmic preferences); (c) creators report feeling "forced" to optimize; (d) community feedback shifts toward "the platform wants us to chase numbers."
- **If algorithmic injustice visibility increases harm:** Within 1-2 weeks, we should see: (a) increased mentions of "unfair," "rigged," or "disadvantaged" in community discussions from creators in lower-benchmark groups; (b) creators from underrepresented groups reporting demoralization; (c) potential increases in complaints or support tickets from these cohorts.

*Indicators of preventability and remediability:*
- **Comparison anxiety:** Largely **preventable** through design (opt-in peer comparison, framing, mental health resources). **Partially remediable** if harms occur (therapy, feature pause). But note: some harm may persist even with mitigations because the feature exists and some users will engage regardless of warnings.
- **Metric-chasing behavior:** **Moderately preventable** through design (counter-metrics, deprioritizing suggestions, framing). But **difficult to fully prevent** because metrics are inherently salient once shown. **Partially remediable** if harm occurs (pausing the feature, introducing friction).
- **Algorithmic injustice visibility:** **Not preventable** via feature design alone (the injustice exists independent of the dashboard). **Not remediable** unless the platform commits to closing algorithmic gaps. However, **partially reducible** through transparency and equity support (reframing the injustice as a problem to solve, not a fact to accept).

*Key uncertainties:*
- We do not know the exact prevalence of comparison sensitivity in our creator base.

### Dimension 3 — HOW FAIR IS IT?

**Spectrum 1: Certain people or groups are affected more than others ←→ All people and groups are affected equally**

*Note: The preamble states "the first spectrum is neutral — neither equal nor unequal distribution is inherently better." This is true. However, in the context of this feature, unequal distribution is the issue under examination.*

**Assessment: Strongly Negative** (impacts are highly unequal across groups)

*Position:* The dashboard's impacts are **severely unequal** across creator demographics, age, and audience size.

**Benefit distribution:**
- Established creators (>50k followers): high benefit
- Growing creators (5k-50k followers): moderate benefit
- Emerging creators (<5k followers): low to negative benefit

**Harm distribution:**
- Younger creators (<25): high harm (comparison anxiety)
- Older creators (>25): moderate harm or none
- Algorithmically-favored categories: lower harm from injustice visibility
- Algorithmically-disadvantaged categories: high harm from injustice visibility

**Fairness assessment:**
- **Not equal:** ~30% of creators benefit greatly; ~35% experience moderate benefit; ~35% experience net harm or no benefit.
- **Directionally aligned with privilege:** Creators who already have reach advantage benefit; creators who lack reach advantage see their disadvantage made visible.

**Observable indicators of unequal impact:**
- Engagement metrics by cohort (established vs. emerging): established creators increase posting frequency, emerging creators decrease
- Comparison anxiety reports by age: younger users report 2-3x higher anxiety than older users
- Community sentiment by creator category: underrepresented categories show more "unfair," "rigged," or "demoralized" language

**Spectrum 2: Those who are harmed are also less advantaged in society; those who benefit have privilege ←→ No more harm to the less advantaged; those who benefit have greater need**

**Assessment: Strongly Negative** (harms concentrate in less advantaged, benefits in privileged)

*Position:* This is **exactly the pattern we're seeing** — a clear fairness problem:

**Those benefiting are already privileged:**
- Established creators (already have large audiences, platform advantage)
- Creators in algorithmic-majority demographics (already receive reach advantage)
- Older, more socially-confident users (less vulnerable to comparison anxiety)

**Those harmed are less advantaged:**
- Emerging creators (fighting against lack of reach, most need help, not discouragement)
- Younger users (more vulnerable to social anxiety, less developed self-concept for comparison)
- Creators in underrepresented demographics (already facing algorithmic disadvantage, now seeing it made visible)

**The problem:** The feature gives a visibility advantage to those already advantaged and gives a demoralizing visibility of disadvantage to those already disadvantaged.

**Example scenario:**
- Creator A (established, majority demographic, age 35): Sees peer benchmarks showing strong engagement. Interprets as validation. Motivation increases. Benefit.
- Creator B (emerging, underrepresented demographic, age 20): Sees peer benchmarks showing lower engagement. Interprets as judgment on own work. Motivation decreases. Harm. Sees average engagement is lower for creators in their category. Internalizes as "the platform is against people like me."

**Is the distribution acceptable or justifiable?**

No. Concentrating benefits in already-privileged groups and harms in less-advantaged groups **violates a basic principle of justice** — that interventions should at minimum not widen existing inequality, and ideally should reduce it. This feature does the opposite: it widens the gap by celebrating the already-successful and demoralizing the struggling.

**Partial justification attempted (and rejected):**
- One might argue: "The feature helps whoever engages with it; it's not unfair, it's just that different people have different uses for the feature." 
- But this ignores that the feature **actively harms** less-advantaged creators through comparison anxiety and visible injustice, not just failing to help them. It's not neutral — it's actively unfair.

*What would a fair alternative look like?*
- The feature would expand benefits to emerging creators (e.g., mentorship paired with metrics, content feedback, equity support)
- The feature would not amplify the visibility of algorithmic disadvantage without addressing the disadvantage itself
- The feature would include safeguards for less-advantaged users (opt-in, mental health support, community validation)

**Spectrum 3: The burden faced by those most harmed is not acceptable or justifiable ←→ The burden is acceptable or justifiable given the broader context**

**Assessment: Strongly Negative** (burden is not acceptable or justifiable)

*Position:* The **burden on harmed groups is not justified** by the context:

**What is the burden?**
- For younger users: psychological distress (anxiety, lowered self-worth) in a critical developmental period
- For emerging creators: demotivation when they most need encouragement to keep creating
- For underrepresented creators: visible confirmation of systemic disadvantage without remedy

**Is it justified?**
- **Is there a compelling societal benefit that overrides individual burden?** No. The feature is a nice-to-have for established creators, not a critical platform need. There's no overriding societal benefit that justifies psychological harm to vulnerable youth.
- **Is the burden necessary?** No. The team has already identified design alternatives (opt-in, mental health support, counter-metrics) that could reduce harm while preserving benefit. The full harm is not inevitable.
- **Is the burden distributed fairly?** No (spectrum 2 above already established it's unfairly concentrated in less-advantaged groups).
- **Is there a path to remedy?** The platform has proposed equity support and algorithmic transparency, but these are supplementary, not remedies. The underlying harms exist independent of these programs.

**What would make the burden acceptable?**
The burden might become more acceptable if:
1. The feature were redesigned so that benefits extended more equitably (e.g., mentorship + metrics for emerging creators)
2. Harms were fully mitigated through design (comparison anxiety reduced to near-zero through opt-in and framing)
3. The platform committed to and demonstrated progress on closing algorithmic gaps (so the burden of seeing injustice comes with visible remediation)
4. The feature included proactive support for harmed groups (not just "here's the harm," but "here's how we're helping")

**In the current design, the burden is not acceptable.**

*Observable indicators of acceptable vs. unacceptable burden:*
- If comparison anxiety affects 5-10% of users with minimal severity: potentially acceptable
- If comparison anxiety affects 20-40% of younger users with moderate-to-high severity: not acceptable without major redesign
- If emerging creators show decreased posting frequency post-launch: not acceptable; this signals demotivation in the group that most needs encouragement
- If creators in underrepresented groups report demoralizing experiences without corresponding support: not acceptable

**Dimension 3 Summary Reflection:**

The feature **fails the fairness test** across all three spectra. Impacts are unequal, unequally distributed to favor privileged groups, and the burden on less-advantaged groups is not justified.

**Critical question:** Does the platform have a responsibility to avoid launching a feature that widens inequality, even if the feature is "optional" or "helpful for some"? Yes. Platforms have responsibilities to consider distributional fairness, especially when launching features that make injustice visible or amplify disadvantage.

The team should redesign to actively support less-advantaged creators, or pause the feature.

### Dimension 4 — HOW EMPOWERING IS IT?

**Spectrum 1: People's ability to make informed choices for themselves is reduced ←→ Ability to make informed choices is unimpaired or increased**

**Assessment: Mixed to Positive** (empowerment depends on user type and design details)

*Position:*
- **Best case (established creators, opt-in peer comparison, strong framing):** The dashboard **increases informed choice**. Creators see data they didn't have before and can make deliberate decisions about content strategy, based on evidence rather than intuition.
- **Worst case (younger users, default peer comparison, weak framing):** The dashboard **reduces informed choice** by creating psychological pressure toward metric-chasing without the user choosing that path. They make choices, but the choices are constrained by anxiety and salience of metrics rather than by their own values.
- **Average case:** The dashboard provides information (positive for choice) but that information comes with implicit pressure (negative for choice). Net effect is roughly neutral to slightly positive, depending on how the feature is framed and which users are in scope.

**Observable indicators of enhanced choice:**
- Creators report using the data to make intentional decisions: "I saw my engagement was lower on Mondays, so I shifted my posting time" (evidence of informed choice)
- Creators report feeling more control over their success: "I understand now what affects my reach" (autonomy increase)
- Creators experiment with different strategies and report learning: "I tried a new format based on the engagement data" (evidence of informed choice in action)

**Observable indicators of reduced choice:**
- Creators report feeling compelled to optimize for metrics even when it conflicts with their values: "I know I should post diverse content, but the dashboard shows X gets more engagement, so I just keep posting X" (choice constrained by salience)
- Creators report anxiety or decision paralysis: "I don't know what to post anymore; I'm too focused on the numbers" (choice paralyzed by information overload and pressure)
- Younger users avoid the feature or report it makes them second-guess their creative instincts: "After seeing the metrics, I don't trust my own judgment anymore" (choice undermined by external judgment)

*Design factor — opt-in matters:*
- If peer comparison is **opt-in**, users who feel it constrains their choice can avoid it, preserving autonomy.
- If peer comparison is **default**, users experience it whether they want to or not, reducing autonomy.

**Reflection — Measurement:**
- **Key metric:** Does the feature correlate with creator decision-making becoming more externally-referenced (data-driven) or more anxiety-driven vs. values-driven? This requires qualitative interviews, not just metrics.
- **Baseline question:** Measure creators' sense of autonomy and control *before* and *after* feature launch. "How much control do you feel you have over your success on this platform?" Scale before and after.

**Spectrum 2: People's control over aspects of their lives is removed ←→ Control is retained or enhanced**

**Assessment: Negative to Mixed** (control is compromised for some, preserved for others)

*Position:*
- **Control over narrative:** Currently, creators can tell themselves "I make the content I want to make, and whatever reach happens, happens." The dashboard removes this control: now the narrative is "my reach is determined by metrics, which are determined by algorithmic factors, which I can't control." This is **more truthful**, but it **reduces perceived control** and could increase learned helplessness (especially in creators facing algorithmic disadvantage).

- **Control over content strategy:** The feature offers creators control to "optimize your approach based on metrics." However, in doing so, it removes control over the creative process. Creators become constrained by what the metrics say works, rather than free to follow their creative instinct. This is a **trade of control**: gain control over strategy optimization, lose control over authentic creative expression.

- **Control over exposure to comparison:** Currently, creators choose whether to engage in comparison (they can avoid thinking about other creators' engagement). With the dashboard, if peer comparison is default, creators lose control over whether they're exposed to comparison. (This is mitigated if peer comparison is opt-in.)

- **Control over algorithmic reach:** Underlying all of this is the fact that creators have very limited control over algorithmic reach. The dashboard makes visible a problem (the reach is determined by algorithmic factors) but does not give creators control over those factors. This can feel like "you're now aware of what you can't control," which could reduce perceived control.

*Observable indicators of control loss:*
- Creators report feeling "at the mercy of the algorithm" after viewing the dashboard (sentiment analysis)
- Creators report feeling they have less creative freedom because they're focused on metrics (qualitative interviews)
- Creators report feeling helpless when they see they're in a low-benchmark category and can't change the category (especially for demographic-based categories)
- Creator autonomy/control scores on validated scales decrease post-feature launch (survey data)

*Observable indicators of control retained/enhanced:*
- Creators report feeling more able to shape their strategy: "I can see what works for me and adjust" (qualitative)
- Creators report increased agency: "I feel like I understand what I can control" (qualitative, autonomy scales)
- Creators who initially felt confused about their engagement now feel they have a framework to operate within (perceived control increase)

**Reflection — The Control Paradox:**
There's a fundamental tension here: **Showing creators the truth about algorithmic reach (transparency) can reduce their perceived control even while giving them more information to work with.** The more creators understand that reach is algorithmic (not merit-based), the more they may feel they lack control, even if information increases slightly.

This argues for **pairing transparency with agency**: don't just show creators what the algorithm does; also show them what they can change. "These factors affect your reach: [list]. Here's what you can control: [specific actions]. Here's what you can't control: [systemic factors; we're working on these]."

**Spectrum 3: There are activities our creation removes the freedom to do ←→ Creation does not coerce, manipulate, or pressure people; limits others' ability to do so**

**Assessment: Negative** (the feature removes freedom through psychological pressure and saliency)

*Position:*
- **Coercion:** The feature does not technically coerce (no one is forced to view it). However, **norm-setting pressure** is strong: if the feature becomes social norm on the platform, creators feel they "should" view it to stay competitive, creating de facto coercion.

- **Manipulation:** The feature **manipulates through saliency.** Metrics are made salient, which automatically captures attention and shifts prioritization. Research in behavioral psychology is clear: **whatever metric you show, people optimize for it.** This is a form of manipulation — the platform is shaping behavior through the choice of what to highlight, not through explicit coercion.

- **Psychological pressure:** The feature creates psychological pressure through:
  - **Comparison:** Peer benchmarks activate social comparison instinct, which is hard to ignore even if the creator rationally intends to ignore them.
  - **Gamification:** Metrics create a game-like structure (here's your score, here's the average), which activates goal-seeking behavior even without explicit incentive.
  - **Loss aversion:** Creators see they're "below average," which activates loss-aversion psychology, driving behavior change to avoid the loss.

These are **soft coercions** — not mandated, but difficult to resist.

*Is this coercion/manipulation justified?*

Some might argue: "All product design includes psychological influence. Notifications are salient, recommendations are salient. This is no different."

**The distinction:** Regular product features create saliency around **product use** (notifications, recommendations); this feature creates saliency around **comparative self-judgment.** The former is optional (users can turn off notifications); the latter, once people know the metrics exist, is harder to ignore because it touches identity and status.

Moreover, the **documented psychological harms** (comparison anxiety, compulsive behavior, reduced autonomy) show that this particular form of saliency has documented negative effects.

**Freedom removed:**
- The freedom to remain unaware of how one's reach compares to peers
- The freedom to pursue authentic creative goals without awareness of metric alternatives
- The freedom to ignore algorithmic logic and create on instinct
- The freedom (for younger users) to avoid social comparison in a critical developmental period

*Observable indicators of removed freedom:*
- Creators report they can't unsee the metrics; they're always aware of them even when not actively viewing (qualitative)
- Creators report they can't not optimize for metrics because "everyone else is" (norm pressure)
- Younger users report they feel unable to avoid the comparison feature; it's expected behavior (qualitative)
- Behavioral change toward metric-optimization even in creators who report wanting to maintain authenticity (behavioral analysis: do creators' actual posting shift toward metric-optimized content even when they say they want to stay authentic?)

*Could autonomy limits be justified by preventing greater harm?*

This is the key question. If the feature prevented greater harm, its autonomy limits might be justified. For example, if showing metrics prevented creators from wasting effort on ineffective strategies, the autonomy trade-off might be worth it.

**However:** The research suggests the opposite — the feature may cause greater harm (comparison anxiety, metric-chasing behavior) than it prevents. So the autonomy limits are **not justified by harm prevention.**

---

**Dimension 4 Summary Reflection:**

The feature **compromises autonomy and freedom** through psychological pressure, saliency effects, and norm-setting, without corresponding harm prevention that would justify these autonomy limits.

**Key tension:** More information ≠ more autonomy. In fact, when information is salient and activates psychological pressure, it can **reduce autonomy** even while increasing information access. This feature shows creators more information (positive) but in a way that constrains their freedom (negative).

**Actionable insight:** To preserve empowerment, the design must ensure:
1. Peer comparison is truly opt-in, not default (so creators retain freedom to avoid it)
2. Framing explicitly names what creators can and cannot control (so information leads to agency, not learned helplessness)
3. Counter-metrics exist (so creators have options for what to optimize toward, not just one salient metric)
4. Mental health resources are paired with the feature (so creators who feel psychologically pressured have support)
