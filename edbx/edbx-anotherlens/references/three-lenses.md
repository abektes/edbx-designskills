# Three Lenses — Full Reflection Question Set

This document contains the complete question set for all three lenses, with facilitation notes for solo and team use.

---

## Lens 1: Balance Your Bias (Green)

Turn your gaze inward. Before examining the product, examine yourself.

### Question 1: What are my lenses? Am I just confirming my assumptions or am I challenging them?

Name your lenses specifically. "I studied at a university" is a lens. "I've never experienced housing insecurity" is a lens. "I'm comfortable with technology" is a lens. The goal is not to list every possible identity factor — it's to identify the lenses most likely to shape this specific design decision.

**Solo facilitation:** Write your lenses down. Read them back. Which ones have you never named before? Which ones feel uncomfortable to write?

**Team facilitation:** Give each person 2 minutes of silent writing. Then share around. Do not let anyone skip — even "I don't think I have strong lenses" is a lens worth naming. Look for patterns: does the team share the same lenses? That's a signal, not a coincidence.

### Question 2: What details here are unfair? Unjustified? Unused?

Scan the design for decisions that can't be defended when examined closely. "Just the way we do it" is not a defense. Neither is "users expect it" without evidence.

**Solo facilitation:** Walk through the design element by element. For each, ask: "Why is this here?" If the answer is circular ("because we designed it that way") or appeals to convention without justification, flag it.

**Team facilitation:** Have each person flag one element they find unjustified. Disagreement here is healthy — it means different lenses are active.

### Question 3: Am I holding on to something that I need to let go of?

This targets sunk costs and emotional attachment. Designers hold on to ideas, layouts, flows, and metaphors long after they've stopped serving users. The question is not whether you like it — it's whether it earns its place.

**Solo facilitation:** Name the thing you'd be most reluctant to remove. Now ask why. If the reason is about you (pride, effort invested, personal preference), it's something to let go of.

**Team facilitation:** This question can feel vulnerable in a group. Consider having people write their answer privately first, then share if willing. Model it: the facilitator goes first.

### Question 4: What's here that I designed for me? What's here that I designed for other people?

The gap between self-design and other-design is where bias lives. Most designs contain both, and the mix reveals priorities.

**Solo facilitation:** Make two columns. For each major design decision, place it in one column. The ratio tells you something. So do the items in the "for me" column — they're often the ones you're most attached to.

**Team facilitation:** Use sticky notes on a wall. One color for "designed for me/us," another for "designed for them." The visual pattern is powerful — teams often discover their "for them" category is thinner than they expected.

## Lens 2: Consider the Opposite (Purple)

Flip the perspective outward. Ask not "what do I think?" but "what would someone who disagrees with me think?"

### Question 1: Who might be impacted by what I'm designing?

Expand the circle beyond direct users. Consider indirect effects: people nearby, people downstream, people excluded, people affected by the design's existence even if they never touch it.

**Solo facilitation:** Draw concentric circles. Inner ring: direct users. Middle ring: indirect users, support staff, moderators, content creators. Outer ring: non-users affected by the design's presence in the world (e.g., people photographed by a product they don't use, communities shaped by a platform they're not on).

**Team facilitation:** Have each person add one group to each ring. The outer ring is usually where the surprises live. Don't stop at "we already considered that" — the goal is completeness, not novelty.

### Question 2: What would the world look like if my assumptions were wrong?

This is a thought experiment in world-building. If the core assumption is wrong, what does reality look like for the people living in it? This is not hypothetical — it's describing the actual experience of someone the design currently fails.

**Solo facilitation:** Write a brief narrative: "If [assumption] is wrong, then [person] experiences [specific thing]." Make it concrete. Not "users would be frustrated" but "a parent at a library computer with 15 minutes and a slow connection would see a blank loading screen and leave without the information they needed."

**Team facilitation:** Have each person write one narrative. Read them aloud. The specificity should make the room quieter — that's the sound of perspective shifting.

### Question 3: Who's someone I'm nervous to talk to about this?

This is the most powerful question in the entire method. The nervousness is the signal. If no one makes you nervous, you haven't looked hard enough.

**This is a required output of every Lens 2 session.**

**Solo facilitation:** Sit with the discomfort. The person you're nervous to talk to is not your enemy — they're the person whose perspective would most challenge your assumptions. Name them specifically, not as a category. "Someone with a disability" is a category. "My neighbor who uses a screen reader and has told me she finds most apps unusable" is a person.

**Team facilitation:** This question can surface real interpersonal dynamics. Create safety by having people write privately first. Share voluntarily. If someone names a colleague or stakeholder, treat it as data about the design process, not a personal confession. The nervousness points to a perspective the design needs.

### Question 4: What do I believe? Who might disagree with what I'm designing?

Separate belief from design decision. Find the people at the intersection — those who hold different beliefs and are affected by the design.

**Solo facilitation:** List your core beliefs about this project (e.g., "I believe this feature helps people stay informed"). Now list who might disagree. Track the disagreement to its source — is it about facts, values, or priorities?

**Team facilitation:** Map beliefs on one axis and potential disagreements on another. Where they intersect is the space for the richest conversation.

## Lens 3: Embrace a Growth Mindset (Orange)

Look forward. Take whatever discomfort surfaced in Lenses 1 and 2 and treat it as material for growth, not evidence of failure.

### Question 1: What am I challenging as I create this? Is my audience open to change?

Connect the work to its context. Not just what's being challenged in the design, but whether the environment — the team, the org, the users — allows the challenge to land.

**Solo facilitation:** Name what you're challenging (a convention, an assumption, a power dynamic). Then assess the audience's readiness honestly. If they're not open, the question becomes: how do you create the conditions for openness?

**Team facilitation:** Discuss as a group. Teams often disagree about the audience's openness — that disagreement is itself useful data.

### Question 2: If I could learn one thing to help me on this project, what would that one thing be?

Identify a concrete learning gap, not an abstract aspiration. "I want to learn more about accessibility" is vague. "I want to understand how screen reader users navigate multi-step forms" is a learning intention you can act on.

**Solo facilitation:** Be specific. The one thing should be learnable within a sprint — through a conversation, a piece of research, or direct observation. If it would take months, break it down.

**Team facilitation:** Each person names their one thing. Look for overlaps — if three people want to learn the same thing, that's a priority. If everyone wants to learn something different, the team may have a knowledge distribution problem.

### Question 3: How does my approach to this problem today compare with how I might have approached this one year ago?

Track growth over time. If your approach hasn't changed, that's worth noticing — it might mean stability, or it might mean stagnation.

**Solo facilitation:** Describe how you would have tackled this problem a year ago. Now describe today's approach. What shifted? What triggered the shift? If nothing shifted, ask whether the problem is the same or whether your thinking has been static.

**Team facilitation:** This question works best with experienced team members who can track their own evolution. For newer designers, reframe: "How does our team's approach today compare with how a team you admire might approach this?"

### Question 4: How can I reframe a mistake in a way that helps me learn?

The reframe engine. Every Lens 1 and 2 discovery can be reframed from "I was wrong" to "I learned something." This is not spin — it's a genuine shift in orientation.

**Solo facilitation:** Take the most uncomfortable thing that surfaced in Lenses 1 or 2. Write it as "I was wrong about [X]." Now rewrite it as "I learned that [Y]." The learning should be specific, actionable, and forward-moving.

**Team facilitation:** Pair up. Each person shares their reframe. The partner's job is to check: is this a genuine reframe or a rebranding of the same mistake? Genuine reframes change what happens next.

### Question 5: Do I need to slow down?

A pacing check. Rushed design compresses the space for reflection. Sometimes the most responsible action is to slow down.

**Solo facilitation:** Be honest. Is the timeline serving the design or serving a deadline? Would one more day of reflection meaningfully change the outcome? If yes, that day is not a luxury — it's a responsibility.

**Team facilitation:** Ask the group: "If we had one more week, what would we do with it?" If the answer is "think more about [X]," then [X] is what needed more time all along.

## Sequencing Notes

- **Quick pass (15 min):** Lens 2 alone. Q3 is the anchor.
- **Standard session (45 min):** All three lenses, 15 minutes each. Skip deep probes.
- **Deep session (60+ min):** All three lenses with follow-up probes and team discussion per question.
- **Return visits:** After applying the lenses once, return to Lens 1 with whatever surfaced in Lens 3. Growth creates new lenses — check them too.
