# Bias Taxonomy for Designers

A reference of common biases that shape design decisions. Use this to help name what surfaces during Lens 1 (Balance Your Bias). Naming a bias is the first step to checking it — you cannot question what you cannot identify.

---

## Cognitive Biases in Design Decisions

### Confirmation Bias
Seeking, interpreting, and remembering information that confirms what you already believe. In design: running user research that validates your existing direction rather than testing it. Asking leading questions. Ignoring feedback that contradicts your hypothesis.

**Design example:** You believe users want a dashboard, so you conduct interviews that ask "what would you want on a dashboard?" instead of "how do you currently track this information?"

### Affinity Bias
Gravitating toward people, ideas, and solutions that feel familiar or similar to you. In design: designing for people like you because it's comfortable. Prioritizing feedback from users who remind you of yourself. Defaulting to aesthetics and patterns from your own cultural context.

**Design example:** Your team is all in their 20s, so you design an onboarding flow optimized for digital natives and call it "intuitive" without testing with older adults.

### Availability Bias
Overweighting information that comes to mind easily — usually because it's recent, vivid, or personal. In design: letting a single dramatic user complaint reshape a product. Designing around the last article you read rather than the full evidence base.

**Design example:** You read an article about voice interfaces being the future, so you redesign your product's primary interaction model around voice without checking whether your users can use voice in their context.

### Anchoring Bias
Relying too heavily on the first piece of information encountered when making decisions. In design: the first wireframe becomes the default layout. The first persona becomes the canonical user. The first metric becomes the only metric.

**Design example:** The first design mock showed the navigation on the left, so every subsequent iteration keeps it there — even when user testing suggests a different configuration.

### Sunk Cost Fallacy
Continuing a course of action because of previously invested resources (time, money, emotional energy), even when evidence suggests it should change. In design: keeping a feature because the team spent three months building it. Defending a direction because you presented it to leadership.

**Design example:** The team invested six weeks in a custom animation system. User testing shows it adds no value and slightly increases load time. The team keeps it anyway because "we already built it."

### Default Effect Bias
Assuming that the current or default state is correct or optimal. In design: treating existing patterns, industry standards, or inherited designs as automatically justified. Not questioning defaults because they feel inevitable.

**Design example:** Your form collects a phone number because the old form did. No one on the team can explain why a phone number is needed, but it stays.

### IKEA Effect
Overvaluing things you helped create. In design: loving your own prototypes disproportionately. Resisting feedback on work you feel ownership over. Confusing "I made this" with "this is good."

**Design example:** The designer who created the original navigation system defends it in every review, even when three rounds of testing show users are confused by it.

### Planning Fallacy
Underestimating the time, cost, and complexity of a project. In design: promising features that require fundamental architecture changes in a single sprint. Assuming edge cases are rare when they're structural.

**Design example:** The team estimates two weeks to add internationalization because "it's just translation," without accounting for text direction, date formats, cultural differences in iconography, or legal requirements.

### Dunning-Kruger Effect
Overestimating your competence in areas where you have limited knowledge. In design: believing you understand a domain because you spent a day reading about it. Making accessibility decisions without consulting people with disabilities. Designing for cultures you've never engaged with.

**Design example:** After reading two articles about designing for older adults, a designer reconfigures the typography and declares the product "age-inclusive" without testing with anyone over 65.

### Framing Effect
Drawing different conclusions from the same information depending on how it's presented. In design: "90% of users complete the flow" sounds like success; "10% of users fail" sounds like failure. They are the same number. The frame shapes the response.

**Design example:** User testing report says "most participants found the checkout easy." The actual data: 7 of 10 participants completed checkout, 2 abandoned, 1 required moderator assistance. The "most" frame obscures the 30% who struggled.

### Bandwagon Effect
Adopting beliefs, patterns, or practices because many others do. In design: using a design pattern because "everyone does it this way." Adding a feature because competitors have it. Following trends without connecting them to user needs.

**Design example:** Adding a dark mode because every app has dark mode now, without checking whether your users work in environments where dark mode helps or hinders.

## Structural Biases in Design Teams

### Demographic Homogeneity
When a team shares demographic characteristics (age, education, culture, ability, socioeconomic background), their shared blind spots become invisible because no one on the team notices them. This is not about blame — it's about the limits of any single perspective.

**What to check:** Does your team reflect the diversity of your user base? Where it doesn't, what perspectives are structurally absent?

### Organizational Bias
Designs shaped by organizational incentives, pressures, and power structures rather than user needs. Features exist because an executive requested them. Metrics are optimized because they're measurable, not because they matter. Timelines compress because of external deadlines, not design readiness.

**What to check:** Which design decisions serve the organization? Which serve the user? Where do those conflict, and who wins?

### Expertise Bias
Experts forget what it's like to not know something. In design: the curse of knowledge. The team's familiarity with the product makes them blind to how a new user experiences it. Complex interfaces feel "simple" to the people who built them.

**What to check:** When did you last watch someone use this product for the first time, without helping them?

### Proximity Bias
Designing for the people closest to you — geographically, culturally, socially — because they're easiest to reach. In design: conducting user research only with people in your city, your network, or your language. Confusing access with representation.

**What to check:** Who is easiest for you to talk to? Who is hardest? The people hardest to reach may be the people most affected by your design.

## Using This Taxonomy

When Lens 1 surfaces something, help the designer name it. "I assumed everyone has reliable internet" could be availability bias (you have reliable internet, so it comes to mind as universal), affinity bias (you're designing for people like you), or default effect (the previous version assumed it, so you did too). The name doesn't need to be perfect — it needs to be specific enough to check.

If none of the named biases fit, name a new one. The taxonomy is a starting point, not an exhaustive list. The act of naming is what matters.
