# Opposite Perspectives — Who Is Typically Absent from Design Research

A reference for Lens 2 (Consider the Opposite). Use this to identify populations whose perspectives are often missing from design decisions, and to find practical ways to reach them.

---

## Who Is Typically Absent

### People with Disabilities
Routinely designed around rather than designed with. Accessibility is often treated as a compliance checklist rather than a design perspective. The result: products that technically meet standards but still feel hostile or cumbersome to use.

**How to reach them:** Partner with disability organizations. Hire disabled consultants. Test with assistive technology users — not simulated, actual. Attend events in the disability community. Pay participants fairly.

**What their perspective reveals:** Assumptions about input methods, timing, visual hierarchy, cognitive load, and what "normal" interaction looks like.

### Older Adults
Frequently excluded from user research due to recruitment bias toward young, tech-savvy participants. When included, they're often treated as a monolithic "seniors" category rather than individuals with diverse experiences and capabilities.

**How to reach them:** Community centers, libraries, senior centers. Religious communities. Volunteer organizations. Family networks — ask younger participants to bring a parent or grandparent. Phone and mail outreach for those less responsive to online recruitment.

**What their perspective reveals:** Assumptions about motor precision, visual acuity, familiarity with digital conventions, pacing, and the relationship between technology and trust.

### People with Low Literacy or Low Digital Literacy
Often invisible in research because recruitment itself requires digital literacy and comfortable reading. Products designed for "everyone" are frequently designed for people who read fluently and navigate interfaces confidently.

**How to reach them:** Adult education programs, community colleges, ESL classes. Social service agencies. Public libraries with digital literacy programs. Work through trusted intermediaries — teachers, social workers, community organizers.

**What their perspective reveals:** Assumptions about language complexity, icon interpretation, form completion, error recovery, and what "intuitive" actually means.

### People Experiencing Poverty
Products that assume stable housing, reliable devices, consistent internet, and financial buffers systematically exclude people in economic precarity. Free-tier products often include the most exploitative patterns, targeting the people least able to opt out.

**How to reach them:** Food banks, shelters, community health centers, legal aid offices. Work with social workers who already have trust-based relationships. Compensate participants in cash or gift cards — not product credits. Remove every barrier to participation (transportation, childcare, scheduling).

**What their perspective reveals:** Assumptions about device quality, connectivity, storage space, payment methods, time availability, and the cost of friction.

### People in Non-Western or Non-English Contexts
Design patterns, visual conventions, color meanings, and interaction models are culturally specific. What "works" in San Francisco may confuse, offend, or exclude in Jakarta, Nairobi, or São Paulo. Yet most design research is conducted in English, in Western contexts, with Western participants.

**How to reach them:** Local research partners who understand the cultural context. Translation that goes beyond words — idioms, metaphors, and visual conventions don't translate directly. Community organizations and local universities. Diaspora communities as a bridge when direct access is difficult.

**What their perspective reveals:** Assumptions about color meaning, icon interpretation, reading direction, formality conventions, privacy expectations, and individual vs. collective orientation.

### People in Rural Areas
Designs optimized for urban contexts — always-on connectivity, short delivery distances, high device density — fail differently in rural contexts. Slow or absent internet, different transportation infrastructure, and different social structures shape how products are used.

**How to reach them:** Agricultural organizations, rural post offices, community gatherings, school networks. Satellite internet communities. Don't simulate rural conditions — go there, or work with people who are there.

**What their perspective reveals:** Assumptions about bandwidth, delivery, service availability, density, and the relationship between physical and digital infrastructure.

### Caregivers
People who provide care for children, elderly family members, or disabled individuals often have constrained time, divided attention, and different priorities than the "typical user." Products designed for undivided attention and flexible schedules fail them.

**How to reach them:** Parent groups, caregiver support organizations, pediatrician offices, elder care facilities. Schedule research at convenient times. Allow interruptions. Pay for childcare during sessions.

**What their perspective reveals:** Assumptions about attention span, uninterrupted use, time of day, multitasking, and what "completion" looks like.

### People Who Are Not Your Users
Some of the most important perspectives come from people who interact with your product indirectly — or who are affected by it without ever using it. Content moderators, delivery drivers, data labelers, community managers, people photographed by your app, people whose neighborhoods change because of your platform.

**How to reach them:** Trace the product's effects beyond the user interface. Who handles the edge cases? Who is in the background of the data? Who lives downstream of the decisions your product automates? Find them through labor organizations, community groups, or direct outreach.

**What their perspective reveals:** Assumptions about who the product is for, where its boundaries are, and who bears the cost of its optimization.

### People Who Left, Complained, or Were Banned
Former users, vocal critics, and people who were removed from a platform hold perspectives that current happy users cannot provide. They know what failure looks like from the inside.

**How to reach them:** Review sites, social media complaints, support ticket themes, exit survey data. Reach out to people who canceled accounts — with genuine curiosity, not a retention pitch. Examine ban and moderation logs for patterns.

**What their perspective reveals:** Where the product breaks down, who it excludes, what the support experience feels like when something goes wrong, and what assumptions the design makes about "correct" behavior.

## Practical Notes on Reaching Absent Perspectives

**Compensation matters.** Pay people fairly for their time and perspective. This is especially important when reaching populations that are not typically part of the design economy. A $200 gift card to a well-paid professional is a nice gesture; the same amount to someone experiencing poverty is meaningful compensation.

**Access matters.** Remove barriers to participation: transportation, childcare, scheduling, language, device requirements, internet speed. If participation requires resources, provide them.

**Trust matters.** Many absent populations have good reasons to distrust research, technology, and institutions. Work through trusted intermediaries. Be transparent about how input will be used. Follow up.

**Power matters.** The designer holds power in a research context — you set the questions, the format, the timing, the interpretation. Be aware of this dynamic and design the research process itself to minimize it.

**Representation vs. tokenism.** Including one person from an underrepresented group does not "check the box." One perspective is one perspective — it's valuable and it's not enough. Do not burden individual participants with the responsibility of representing an entire community.

## Using This Reference

When Lens 2 asks "Who might be impacted?" and the answer only includes people who look like the design team, this list is a prompt to look further. The categories are not exhaustive — they represent common blind spots in design research. The specific populations absent from your research will depend on your context, your product, and your team.

The question is not "did we include diverse perspectives?" The question is "whose perspective would change this design if we heard it?"
