# Values Levers

**The short version:** Identifies what you can actually do — given your specific role and authority — to move your team or organization toward ethical design. Six levers. Realistic about power. Concrete about action.

---

## Who made it

Values Levers comes from **Katie Shilton's** ethnography of a university lab that built mobile-phone sensing software ("Values Levers: Building Ethics into Design", *Science, Technology, & Human Values*, 2013). A values lever is a work practice that opens conversations about social values and builds consensus around them as design criteria. Shilton and Koepfler's later paper adds the link to communication: modes of communication can enable or constrain levers, and levers in turn enable or constrain values-based design decisions. Shilton calls her list of levers probably incomplete and warns that one lab's findings will not hold for every setting.

This skill adapts those findings into a role-sensitive planning method for designers and product managers inside technology organizations. The activation guidance, resistance map, whose-values mapping and 30-day plan are edbx's, not Shilton's.

---

## The problem it solves

Designers often know what the ethical thing to do is. They don't always have the organizational power to do it.

This is the problem Values Levers is designed for. It's not about teaching ethics. It's about identifying the specific mechanisms — given your role, your authority, your organizational context — through which you can shift team behavior toward ethical design.

The method is honest about power. It doesn't ask an individual contributor to do things that require management authority. It doesn't ask a design manager to do things that require executive mandate. It maps what's actually available to you and generates a concrete plan for using it.

---

## When to use it

- **When you want to get your team to care about ethics** — and don't know where to start
- **When ethical concerns you've raised are being ignored** — and you want to find more effective approaches
- **When you're an individual contributor without organizational authority** — but you still want to act
- **When you're a design manager who wants to build ethical design culture** — not just run one-off audits
- **When ethics is deprioritized because it's not on the roadmap** — and you want to find a way to change that
- **When direct advocacy isn't safe** — and you need indirect strategies that still move things forward

## What it produces

Values Levers identifies which of six levers are available and applicable given your role and context. The first five match those Shilton and Koepfler list; Shilton's article adds the sixth. The "how to" guidance under each is edbx's:

**Lever 1 — Interdisciplinary Teams:** Bringing ethics, legal, community representatives, or engineers into design conversations that would otherwise stay siloed in design. Most valuable when values discussions are happening in echo chambers.

**Lever 2 — Designing Around Constraints:** Imposing a constraint that forces the team to confront a value conflict. "Design this feature as if we can't use location data." The constraint makes the trade-off visible in a way that abstract discussion doesn't.

**Lever 3 — Leader/Team Member Advocacy:** Equipping someone with standing — a senior designer, an engineering lead, a product manager — with the language and framing to champion ethical concerns in meetings where they'd otherwise go unspoken.

**Lever 4 — Self-Testing:** Having team members experience their own system as a non-expert, a vulnerable user, or someone from a marginalized context. "Use your app for a week as if you have a motor disability" or "try to delete your account."

**Lever 5 — Gaining/Funding:** Framing ethics investment as risk reduction to access budget, headcount, or roadmap space. "One hour with legal prevents six months of compliance work" is a different conversation than "we should be more ethical." (In Shilton's lab, funding mattered because it paid for larger, more interdisciplinary teams and a long-term values advocate.)

**Lever 6 — Navigating Institutional Mandates:** Working with an outside body that imposes requirements, such as an ethics review board, so that its review keeps values on the agenda. In Shilton's lab, leaders approached the university review board early and valued it as an external check.

For each applicable lever, the method produces:
- **How to activate it** in your specific context
- **The most common organizational resistance** you'll face
- **A specific counter-move** for that resistance

Before the lever activation plan, the skill requires an edbx addition, the **"Whose Values Are Visible — and Whose Aren't" mapping**, which widens the conversation beyond the team's own values (it is not in Shilton's papers):

- **Stated vs. unstated values driving current decisions** — for every stakeholder in the room, both what they say drives them and what actually drives them (risk avoidance, career safety, optionality, plausible deniability, fear of being blamed).
- **Absent stakeholders, named specifically** — at least 3 populations whose values aren't in the conversation: e.g., users with disabilities (would want accessible-by-default), people in 5 years using accumulated user data (would want meaningful data minimization), users in regimes where data could be subpoenaed, etc. For each: what they would want, and how their absence shapes the design.
- **Systemic / second-order harm map** — aggregation effects (what becomes possible when many users' data combines), power dynamics (what the design hands the operator and how reversible), future discriminatory use (protected-class inferences this data enables), non-user externalities (people affected without opting in).

Without this section the output stays at organizational risk level — strong on team alignment, weak on ethical depth.

The output ends with a **30-Day Action Plan** for the top two levers — week-by-week actions with named stakeholders, deliverables, and success/failure indicators.

## Individual Mode

If you're an individual contributor without organizational authority — no direct reports, no budget, no mandate — Values Levers activates a fast path. It focuses on Levers 3 and 4 (advocacy through others, self-testing) and generates actions you can take without anyone's permission.

You don't need organizational power to suggest a constraint exercise. You don't need budget to ask "has anyone tried deleting their account as if they were a new user?" You don't need management buy-in to find a senior ally and give them language and framing for a concern you've already identified.

Individual Mode is for people who want to act now, with what they have.

---

## The key insight

The most common failure mode in ethical design advocacy is asking for too much at once. "We need a full ethics review process" requires organizational buy-in, a roadmap slot, a budget, and someone to own it. It's aspirational and unactionable in a two-week sprint.

"Could we spend 30 minutes in our next retro with everyone using the app as if they have limited data access?" — that's a Lever 4 activation. It requires no budget, no authority, and no culture change. It just needs someone to ask the question.

Values Levers is about finding the smallest action that opens the largest door.

## How to use it with AI

Describe your team context, your role (individual contributor, team lead, design manager), and the specific ethical concern or situation you're trying to change. The AI will assess which levers are available to you, provide activation guidance and resistance counter-moves for each, and produce a 30-day action plan for the top two levers.

## A quick example

**Context:** Individual designer on a platform team. No direct reports. The team uses a notification system the designer believes is creating anxiety and overuse patterns. Leadership hasn't engaged with the concern when raised verbally.

**Levers available:**
- Lever 3 (Advocacy): The design manager is sympathetic but hasn't framed the concern in business terms. Designer can provide framing: "Notification-driven anxiety has been linked to platform churn in competitors' research. Positioning this as a retention risk changes the conversation."
- Lever 4 (Self-Testing): "I'd like to propose we spend 30 minutes in next week's retro all using the app with notifications at maximum frequency for 48 hours and sharing what we notice."

**30-Day Action Plan:**

- **Week 1:** Brief the design manager with the retention-risk framing. Ask if they'd be willing to raise it in the next product planning meeting. Prepare a one-page summary of competitor research on notification-driven churn.
- **Week 2:** Propose the self-testing exercise for the retro. Keep it low-stakes: "Let's just see what we notice." Document what surfaces.
- **Week 3:** Compile self-testing observations into a short internal report. Ask the design manager to share it with the PM as input to the roadmap conversation.
- **Week 4:** Checkpoint — has the notification concern appeared on any roadmap or discussion agenda? If yes, the lever worked. If no, identify the next lever to activate.
