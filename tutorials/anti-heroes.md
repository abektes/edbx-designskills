# Anti-Heroes

**The short version:** Every product has users it was designed for. Anti-Heroes finds the people it wasn't designed for — and maps the harm it causes them anyway.

---

## Who made it

Anti-Heroes was developed by Shruthi Sai Chivukula, Wendy Ju, Colin M. Gray, and colleagues, published in a 2024 research paper (arXiv:2405.03674). It comes out of ongoing academic work on ethical design methods, particularly around who gets left out of design thinking.

The name is intentional. "Anti-heroes" are the people in the story who aren't the heroes — not villains, not antagonists, just people the design didn't account for, and who bear consequences they didn't choose.

---

## The problem it solves

Design thinking centers the user. That's its strength and its blind spot. When you design for a user, you optimize for that user's experience. But products don't exist in isolation — they exist in ecosystems, communities, and social systems where many people are affected by their existence, including people who never consented to be.

The gig worker who delivers your food never signed up to be part of your UX. The teenager who sees their friend's workout streak and feels inadequate didn't install the fitness app. The family member affected by an addict's gambling app isn't a user of anything.

Anti-Heroes is the method that finds those people before the product ships.

---

## When to use it

- **When your design is working as intended but something still feels wrong** — the feature is successful by every metric, and yet
- **Before launch of any product that operates in a social context** — which is almost all products
- **When your team is asking "who does this harm?" and can only name users** — direct users aren't the only people harmed
- **During a feature review** when the team is focused on the primary user journey and hasn't asked about anyone else
- **When the product involves social comparison, labor, attention, data collection, or any platform dynamic** — these are especially high-risk for third-party harm

---

## What it produces

Anti-Heroes works in four modes depending on what you need:

1. **Evaluation Mode** — you have a product or feature; the method finds the anti-heroes and maps what harm they experience
2. **Reverse Brainstorming Mode** — you work backward from a potential harm to find who experiences it
3. **Conceptualization Mode** — you use anti-hero thinking early in the process to shape the design before harm is baked in
4. **Ethical Dialogue Mode** — the method generates questions and scenarios for team discussion rather than a structured audit

The output is a map of **who is harmed, how they are harmed, and what the design would need to change to stop harming them.** It names specific populations, specific mechanisms of harm, and specific design decisions that could be different.

---

## The key insight

The most important distinction Anti-Heroes makes is between:

- **Harms to non-users** — people who are affected by the product's existence but never chose to use it
- **Harms to indirect users** — people who interact with the product through someone else's use of it
- **Systemic harms** — harms that only appear when many people use the product simultaneously

A single user opening a social media app is fine. Ten million users opening it, comparing themselves to each other, and experiencing anxiety and inadequacy — that's a systemic harm that doesn't exist at the individual level.

Anti-Heroes forces you to think at all three levels.

---

## How to use it with AI

Describe your product or feature and who it's designed for. Then ask for an Anti-Heroes analysis. The AI will work through the concentric circles of impact — from direct users outward to non-users, affected communities, and society — mapping who experiences harm and through what mechanism.

You can focus on a specific mode (Evaluation, Reverse Brainstorming, Conceptualization, or Ethical Dialogue) depending on where you are in your process.

---

## A quick example

**Product:** A fitness app with public leaderboards and streak tracking.

**Anti-heroes found:**

- **Non-users who feel social comparison pressure** — friends who don't use the app but hear about streaks and rankings from friends who do, leading to feelings of inadequacy
- **Users recovering from eating disorders** — for whom gamified achievement metrics can trigger relapse behavior (this group may technically be "users" but are harmed despite the feature working as intended)
- **Lower-wage workers** who can't afford consistent gym access and whose absence from leaderboards marks them as less-disciplined rather than less-resourced
- **Partners and family members** of obsessive users — who experience the product's consequences in the form of lost time, disrupted routines, and social pressure

The design fix isn't necessarily to remove leaderboards. It might be to make them opt-in, to show relative improvement rather than absolute ranking, or to allow private streaks. Anti-Heroes surfaces the harm; the redesign direction follows.
